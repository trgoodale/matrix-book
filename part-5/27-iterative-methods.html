27  Iterative methods

Further

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • solve linear systems with the Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel and SOR iterations
  • predict whether and how fast they converge, using diagonal dominance and the spectral radius
  • outline the conjugate gradient method
  • choose between direct and iterative solvers

Builds on Chapter 19, Chapter 26.

Gaussian elimination is a direct method: after a fixed number of steps it gives the answer, up to rounding. For the very large sparse systems of Chapter 26, that fixed number of steps can be too many, because fill-in makes the factors too big to store. Iterative methods take a different approach. They start from a guess and improve it, step after step, using nothing but multiplications by the original sparse matrix, so they never create fill-in. They give an approximate answer, but you choose how accurate, and often a few hundred cheap steps beat one enormous factorisation.

27.1 Build intuition

The app solves two equations in two unknowns by three iterative methods: each starts from a guess and improves it, step by step. It draws each guess as a point in the plane; the solution is where the two lines cross. The methods are called Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel and SOR, and this chapter explains how each one works. For now, just watch what they do.

27.2 The ideas

Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel

Solve the \(i\)th equation for the \(i\)th unknown. For the system \[ \begin{aligned} 4x_1 + x_2 &= 5, \\ 2x_1 + 5x_2 &= 7, \end{aligned} \qquad\text{this gives}\qquad \begin{aligned} x_1 &= (5 - x_2)/4, \\ x_2 &= (7 - 2x_1)/5. \end{aligned} \] The Jacobi method uses these as update rules: put the current guess into the right-hand sides to get the next guess. The Gauss–Seidel method does the same, but uses each new value as soon as it has been computed: the new \(x_1\) is used straight away to compute the new \(x_2\). Gauss–Seidel usually converges faster, and needs only one copy of the vector in memory.

In matrix form, split \(\M{A} = \M{D} + \M{L} + \M{U}\) into its diagonal \(\M{D}\), its strictly lower triangular part \(\M{L}\) and its strictly upper triangular part \(\M{U}\). (These \(\M{L}\) and \(\M{U}\) are not the LU factors.) Then \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Jacobi:}\quad & \M{D}\vect{x}_{k+1} = \vect{b} - (\M{L} + \M{U})\vect{x}_k, \\ \text{Gauss–Seidel:}\quad & (\M{D} + \M{L})\vect{x}_{k+1} = \vect{b} - \M{U}\vect{x}_k. \end{aligned} \] Each step solves a diagonal or triangular system, which is cheap, and multiplies by sparse matrices, which is cheap too.

NoteExample 1: Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel by hand

Take three steps of each method for the system above, starting from \(\vect{x}_0 = (0, 0)\T\). The solution is \((1, 1)\T\).

Solution. Jacobi uses the old values in both formulas: \[ \vect{x}_1 = \mat{5/4 \\ 7/5} = \mat{1.25 \\ 1.4}, \quad \vect{x}_2 = \mat{(5 - 1.4)/4 \\ (7 - 2.5)/5} = \mat{0.9 \\ 0.9}, \quad \vect{x}_3 = \mat{1.025 \\ 1.04}. \] Gauss–Seidel uses the new \(x_1\) at once: \[ \vect{x}_1 = \mat{1.25 \\ (7 - 2.5)/5} = \mat{1.25 \\ 0.9}, \quad \vect{x}_2 = \mat{(5 - 0.9)/4 \\ (7 - 2.05)/5} = \mat{1.025 \\ 0.99}, \quad \vect{x}_3 = \mat{1.0025 \\ 0.999}. \] After three steps, Jacobi’s error is about 0.04 and Gauss–Seidel’s about 0.0025. The matrix is strictly diagonally dominant (\(4 > 1\) and \(5 > 2\)), so both are sure to converge.

When do they converge?

Both methods have the form \(\vect{x}_{k+1} = \M{T}\vect{x}_k + \vect{c}\), for an iteration matrix \(\M{T}\): \(\M{T}_J = -\M{D}^{-1}(\M{L} + \M{U})\) for Jacobi and \(\M{T}_{GS} = -(\M{D} + \M{L})^{-1}\M{U}\) for Gauss–Seidel. The exact solution \(\vect{x}\) satisfies \(\vect{x} = \M{T}\vect{x} + \vect{c}\), so subtracting, the error \(\vect{e}_k = \vect{x}_k - \vect{x}\) obeys \[ \vect{e}_{k+1} = \M{T}\vect{e}_k, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \vect{e}_k = \M{T}^k\vect{e}_0. \] This is a matrix power again (Chapter 19), and its behaviour is set by the eigenvalues of \(\M{T}\). The spectral radius \(\rho(\M{T})\) is the largest of their absolute values.

ImportantConvergence of an iterative method

The iteration converges from every starting guess if and only if \(\rho(\M{T}) < 1\). The error then shrinks by a factor of about \(\rho(\M{T})\) per step, so gaining one more correct digit takes about \(-1/\log_{10}\rho(\M{T})\) steps.

Finding \(\rho(\M{T})\) is as hard as solving the system, so a simple sufficient condition is useful. A matrix is strictly diagonally dominant if, in every row, the diagonal entry is larger in size than the sum of the sizes of the other entries: \[ \lvert a_{ii}\rvert > \sum_{j \neq i}\lvert a_{ij}\rvert \quad\text{for every } i. \] For such matrices, both Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel are sure to converge (Section C.18). Gauss–Seidel also always converges for symmetric positive definite matrices. Reordering the equations can sometimes make a matrix diagonally dominant, as Diverges in the app shows.

NoteExample 2: the spectral radius

Find the iteration matrices for Example 1 and their spectral radii.

Solution. Here \(\M{D} = \mat{4 & 0 \\ 0 & 5}\), \(\M{L} = \mat{0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0}\) and \(\M{U} = \mat{0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0}\). For Jacobi, \[ \M{T}_J = -\M{D}^{-1}(\M{L} + \M{U}) = \mat{0 & -\tfrac{1}{4} \\ -\tfrac{2}{5} & 0}, \] with \(\lambda^2 = \tfrac{1}{10}\), so \(\rho(\M{T}_J) = 1/\sqrt{10} \approx 0.316\). For Gauss–Seidel, \[ \M{T}_{GS} = -(\M{D} + \M{L})^{-1}\M{U} = -\mat{4 & 0 \\ 2 & 5}^{-1}\mat{0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0} = \mat{0 & -\tfrac{1}{4} \\ 0 & \tfrac{1}{10}}, \] which is triangular, with eigenvalues 0 and \(\tfrac{1}{10}\), so \(\rho(\M{T}_{GS}) = 0.1 = \rho(\M{T}_J)^2\). Each Gauss–Seidel step gains one correct digit, and each Jacobi step about half a digit, as Example 1 shows.

Successive over-relaxation

When convergence is slow, Gauss–Seidel’s steps all point the right way but are too short. Successive over-relaxation (SOR) stretches each one by a factor \(\omega\): it computes the Gauss–Seidel value for each unknown and then moves \(\omega\) times as far from the old value, \[ x_i^{\text{new}} = (1 - \omega)x_i^{\text{old}} + \omega\,x_i^{\text{Gauss–Seidel}}. \] With \(\omega = 1\) this is Gauss–Seidel. It converges only for \(0 < \omega < 2\), and the best \(\omega\) is usually between 1 and 2. For many matrices from grid problems, including the examples here, the best value is \[ \omega_{\text{best}} = \frac{2}{1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho(\M{T}_J)^2}}, \] and it can cut the number of steps from thousands to a hundred or so.

NoteExample 3: a larger problem

The tridiagonal system \(-x_{i-1} + 2x_i - x_{i+1} = b_i\) with \(n = 50\) unknowns models the temperature along a heated rod (Chapter 33). How many steps does each method need to reduce the residual by a factor of \(10^6\)?

Solution. For this matrix \(\rho(\M{T}_J) = \cos\bigl(\pi/(n + 1)\bigr) \approx 0.9981\), desperately close to 1. The code below gives:

Method Steps
Jacobi 7227
Gauss–Seidel 3615
SOR with \(\omega_{\text{best}} \approx 1.884\) 150
Conjugate gradients 25

The matrix is only just diagonally dominant (2 is equal to, not greater than, \(1 + 1\) in the middle rows), and its condition number grows like \(n^2\), which is why the simple methods are so slow. For this small problem a direct tridiagonal solve (Chapter 26) is better than any of them; the comparison matters for large two- and three-dimensional problems, where direct methods become too expensive.

Conjugate gradients

For symmetric positive definite systems, the most important iterative method is the conjugate gradient method (CG). Chapter 22 showed that solving \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) is the same as minimising \(f(\vect{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\vect{x}\T\M{A}\vect{x} - \vect{b}\T\vect{x}\), and that gradient descent zigzags when the contours are long, thin ellipses. CG fixes the zigzag.

Each step searches along a direction chosen to be conjugate to all the earlier ones, \(\vect{p}_i\T\M{A}\vect{p}_j = 0\), which means that minimising along the new direction never spoils the minimisation already done along the old ones.

In exact arithmetic it reaches the solution in at most \(n\) steps, and in practice it gets close far sooner: the number of steps needed grows like \(\sqrt{\kappa(\M{A})}\), compared with \(\kappa(\M{A})\) for Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel. Each step costs one multiplication by \(\M{A}\) and a few vector operations.

For matrices that are not symmetric, GMRES and BiCGSTAB play the same role. Together these are called Krylov methods, because they build their answers from the vectors \(\vect{b}, \M{A}\vect{b}, \M{A}^2\vect{b}, \dots\)

When to stop, and which method to use

An iteration needs a stopping rule. The usual one stops when the residual is small compared with the right-hand side, \(\lVert\vect{b} - \M{A}\vect{x}_k\rVert \le \text{tol} \times \lVert\vect{b}\rVert\). Remember that a small residual means a small error only if \(\M{A}\) is well conditioned (Chapter 24).

As a rough guide:

  • For small and medium systems (up to perhaps \(10^4\) unknowns, or \(10^5\) to \(10^6\) if sparse in a two-dimensional pattern), use a direct solver: it is reliable and needs no tuning.
  • For very large sparse systems, especially from three-dimensional problems, use a Krylov method with a good preconditioner (below).
  • Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel and SOR are rarely used alone today, but they are the building blocks of preconditioners and multigrid methods, and they show how all iterative methods behave.

27.3 In code

Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel take only a few lines. For real problems, use the library Krylov solvers: scipy.sparse.linalg.cg and gmres in Python, pcg and gmres in MATLAB.

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_triangular
from scipy.sparse.linalg import cg

n = 50
A = 2 * np.eye(n) - np.eye(n, k=1) - np.eye(n, k=-1)
b = np.ones(n) / (n + 1)**2
D = np.diag(np.diag(A))
L = np.tril(A, -1)
U = np.triu(A, 1)

rho = max(abs(np.linalg.eigvals(np.linalg.solve(D, L + U))))
w = 2 / (1 + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2))
splittings = {"Jacobi": (D, L + U), "Gauss-Seidel": (D + L, U),
              "SOR": (D / w + L, U + (1 - 1 / w) * D)}

for name, (M, N) in splittings.items():
    x = np.zeros(n)
    k = 0
    while np.linalg.norm(b - A @ x) >= 1e-6 * np.linalg.norm(b):
        x = solve_triangular(M, b - N @ x, lower=True)   # M is diagonal or lower triangular
        k += 1
    print(f"{name:13s} {k:5d} steps")

steps = []
x, info = cg(A, b, rtol=1e-6, callback=lambda xk: steps.append(1))
print(f"{'CG':13s} {len(steps):5d} steps")
Jacobi         7227 steps
Gauss-Seidel   3615 steps
SOR             150 steps
CG               25 steps
n = 50;
A = 2*eye(n) - diag(ones(n-1,1), 1) - diag(ones(n-1,1), -1);
b = ones(n, 1) / (n + 1)^2;
D = diag(diag(A));
L = tril(A, -1);
U = triu(A, 1);

rho = max(abs(eig(D \ (L + U))));
w = 2 / (1 + sqrt(1 - rho^2));
names = {'Jacobi', 'Gauss-Seidel', 'SOR'};
M = {D, D + L, D/w + L};
N = {L + U, U, U + (1 - 1/w)*D};

for m = 1:3
    x = zeros(n, 1);
    k = 0;
    while norm(b - A*x) >= 1e-6 * norm(b)
        x = M{m} \ (b - N{m}*x);       % M is diagonal or triangular
        k = k + 1;
    end
    fprintf('%-13s %5d steps\n', names{m}, k);
end

[x, flag, relres, iters] = pcg(A, b, 1e-6, 100);
fprintf('%-13s %5d steps\n', 'CG', iters);

The SOR splitting writes \(\M{D}/\omega + \M{L}\) for the matrix solved at each step, which is the same as the formula in the text. In a serious implementation, the matrices would be sparse and the triangular solves done row by row.

27.4 Check your understanding

TipIs \(\mat{3 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 3}\) strictly diagonally dominant?

Yes: in each row the diagonal entry wins, since \(3 > 1 + 1\), \(4 > 1 + 2\) and \(3 > 0 + 2\).

TipAn iteration has \(\rho(\M{T}) = 0.9\). About how many steps does it need to reduce the error by a factor of \(10^6\)?

About \(6/(-\log_{10}0.9) \approx 131\) steps.

TipWhy don’t iterative methods suffer from fill-in?

They never change the matrix: they only multiply vectors by it (and by its diagonal or triangular parts), so its zeros stay zero.

27.5 Key ideas

  • Iterative methods improve a guess step by step. Jacobi updates every unknown from the old values; Gauss–Seidel uses each new value as soon as it has it.
  • Both converge for every starting guess exactly when the spectral radius of their iteration matrix is less than 1; strict diagonal dominance guarantees this.
  • The closer the spectral radius is to 1, the slower the convergence, and SOR with a well-chosen \(\omega\) can speed it up enormously.
  • For symmetric positive definite systems, the conjugate gradient method is the method of choice.
  • Iterative methods pay off for large sparse systems, where direct elimination would fill in.

27.7 In practice

  • Heat flow, groundwater flow and electrostatics on large two- and three-dimensional grids lead to huge sparse symmetric positive definite systems, usually solved with preconditioned CG.
  • Multigrid methods use a few Jacobi or Gauss–Seidel steps to remove the rapidly varying part of the error, then correct the slowly varying part on a coarser grid. They can solve grid problems in a number of operations proportional to \(n\), the best possible.
  • Computational fluid dynamics codes use GMRES and BiCGSTAB with preconditioners for the non-symmetric systems from flow equations.
  • PageRank and many network calculations are iterative methods applied to enormous sparse matrices (Chapter 35).
  • Each step is parallel: a Jacobi step updates every unknown independently, which suits graphics cards and supercomputers well.

27.8 Going further

  • Proofs that \(\rho(\M{T}) < 1\) is needed for convergence, and that Jacobi converges for strictly diagonally dominant matrices: Section C.18.
  • Preconditioning replaces \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) by \(\M{M}^{-1}\M{A}\vect{x} = \M{M}^{-1}\vect{b}\), where \(\M{M}\) is an approximation to \(\M{A}\) that is easy to solve with, such as its diagonal, or an incomplete LU factorisation that discards fill-in. A good preconditioner makes \(\M{M}^{-1}\M{A}\) close to \(\M{I}\) and so well conditioned, and Krylov methods then converge in a handful of steps.
  • Saad’s Iterative Methods for Sparse Linear Systems (freely available from the author) and Shewchuk’s An Introduction to the Conjugate Gradient Method Without the Agonizing Pain are good next steps.
