30  QR factorisation and stable least squares

Further

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • compute a QR factorisation with Gram–Schmidt and with Householder reflections
  • solve least-squares problems with QR
  • explain why QR is more accurate than the normal equations
  • describe how libraries compute the SVD

Builds on Chapter 17, Chapter 24.

Chapter 17 found the least-squares solution of an overdetermined system \(\M{A}\vect{x} \approx \vect{b}\) from the normal equations \(\M{A}\T\M{A}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{A}\T\vect{b}\). They are easy to derive and fine for small, well-behaved problems, but they have a hidden flaw: forming \(\M{A}\T\M{A}\) squares the condition number, so a problem that should lose 6 digits loses 12. This chapter shows how to avoid that, by factorising \(\M{A} = \M{Q}\M{R}\) with orthogonal \(\M{Q}\). Orthogonal matrices never magnify errors, so QR-based least squares loses only the digits the problem itself demands. It is what lstsq, polyfit and MATLAB’s backslash use.

30.1 Build intuition

There is no app for this chapter. Instead, consider this experiment. Fifty points are generated exactly from the polynomial \(1 + t + t^2 + \dots + t^d\) on \(0 \le t \le 1\), and a polynomial of degree \(d\) is fitted to them by least squares. The right answer is obvious: every coefficient is 1. Here is the largest error in the fitted coefficients, computed in double precision in two ways:

30.2 The ideas

The QR factorisation

ImportantQR factorisation

Every \(m \times n\) matrix \(\M{A}\) with \(m \ge n\) and independent columns can be written as \[ \M{A} = \M{Q}\M{R}, \] where \(\M{Q}\) is \(m \times n\) with orthonormal columns (\(\M{Q}\T\M{Q} = \M{I}\)) and \(\M{R}\) is \(n \times n\), upper triangular and invertible.

This is the reduced QR factorisation. The full one extends \(\M{Q}\) to a square \(m \times m\) orthogonal matrix, and pads \(\M{R}\) with \(m - n\) rows of zeros. The columns of \(\M{Q}\) are an orthonormal basis for the column space of \(\M{A}\), and \(\M{R}\) records how to rebuild each column of \(\M{A}\) from them: since \(\M{R}\) is triangular, column \(j\) of \(\M{A}\) uses only the first \(j\) columns of \(\M{Q}\).

QR by Gram–Schmidt

The Gram–Schmidt process of Chapter 17 produces exactly this factorisation. Working through the columns \(\vect{a}_1, \vect{a}_2, \dots\) in turn, it subtracts from \(\vect{a}_j\) its components along the earlier \(\vect{q}_i\), with coefficients \(r_{ij} = \vect{q}_i \cdot \vect{a}_j\), and normalises what is left, of length \(r_{jj}\). The numbers \(r_{ij}\) form \(\M{R}\).

In floating point, this classical Gram–Schmidt slowly loses orthogonality: when the columns of \(\M{A}\) are nearly dependent, the computed \(\vect{q}_j\) are far from perpendicular. A small rearrangement, modified Gram–Schmidt, subtracts each component as soon as each \(\vect{q}_i\) is found, from all the remaining columns, so that later projections use already-corrected vectors. It is much better, but still not perfect. For the degree-9 fitting matrix above, \(\lVert\M{Q}\T\M{Q} - \M{I}\rVert\) is about \(10^{-2}\) for classical, \(10^{-10}\) for modified, and \(10^{-15}\) for the next method.

Householder reflections

The method libraries use builds \(\M{Q}\) from reflections, which are exactly orthogonal. A Householder reflection is \[ \M{H} = \M{I} - \frac{2\vect{v}\vect{v}\T}{\vect{v}\T\vect{v}}, \] the reflection in the plane (or line, or hyperplane) perpendicular to \(\vect{v}\). It is symmetric and orthogonal, and \(\M{H}^2 = \M{I}\): reflecting twice puts everything back. Given any vector \(\vect{x}\), choosing \[ \vect{v} = \vect{x} + \operatorname{sign}(x_1)\lVert\vect{x}\rVert\,\ue{1} \] gives a reflection that sends \(\vect{x}\) to \(-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\lVert\vect{x}\rVert\,\ue{1}\): a vector along the first axis, of the same length. All entries but the first become zero (Figure 30.1 shows Example 1). (The sign is chosen to avoid cancellation when forming \(\vect{v}\), Chapter 24.)

Householder QR uses such reflections the way elimination uses row operations. The first reflection \(\M{H}_1\) zeros column 1 below the diagonal; the second, acting only on rows 2 to \(m\), zeros column 2 below the diagonal; and so on. After \(n\) reflections, \[ \M{H}_n\cdots\M{H}_2\M{H}_1\M{A} = \mat{\M{R} \\ \M{0}}, \] and \(\M{Q} = \M{H}_1\M{H}_2\cdots\M{H}_n\) (each \(\M{H}_i\) is its own inverse). Because every step is an orthogonal transformation, which preserves lengths, rounding errors are never magnified. Householder QR costs about \(2mn^2 - \tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) operations, roughly twice as much as forming and solving the normal equations.

NoteExample 1: a Householder reflection

Find the Householder reflection that sends \(\vect{x} = (3, 4)\T\) to a multiple of \(\ue{1}\).

Solution. \(\lVert\vect{x}\rVert = 5\) and \(x_1 > 0\), so \(\vect{v} = (3, 4)\T + 5(1, 0)\T = (8, 4)\T\), with \(\vect{v}\T\vect{v} = 80\). Then \[ \M{H} = \M{I} - \frac{2}{80}\mat{64 & 32 \\ 32 & 16} = \mat{-0.6 & -0.8 \\ -0.8 & 0.6}, \qquad \M{H}\vect{x} = \mat{-1.8 - 3.2 \\ -2.4 + 2.4} = \mat{-5 \\ 0}. \] \(\M{H}\) is symmetric, its columns are orthonormal, and its determinant is \(-1\), as for any reflection.

Least squares with QR

Substitute \(\M{A} = \M{Q}\M{R}\) into the normal equations: \[ \M{R}\T\M{Q}\T\M{Q}\M{R}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{R}\T\M{Q}\T\vect{b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \M{R}\T\M{R}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{R}\T\M{Q}\T\vect{b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \M{R}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{Q}\T\vect{b}, \] cancelling the invertible \(\M{R}\T\). So:

ImportantLeast squares by QR

Factorise \(\M{A} = \M{Q}\M{R}\), compute \(\M{Q}\T\vect{b}\), and solve the triangular system \(\M{R}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{Q}\T\vect{b}\) by back substitution.

\(\M{A}\T\M{A}\) is never formed. Why does that matter? Its singular values are the squares of those of \(\M{A}\) (Chapter 21), so \[ \kappa_2(\M{A}\T\M{A}) = \kappa_2(\M{A})^2. \] The normal equations solve a problem with the squared condition number, losing about \(2\log_{10}\kappa(\M{A})\) digits, while QR loses only about \(\log_{10}\kappa(\M{A})\) (strictly, the least-squares problem’s sensitivity also depends on how well the data fit; when the residual is large it can approach \(\kappa^2\), but QR never adds extra loss of its own). In the table above, with \(\kappa(\M{A}) \approx 7 \times 10^8\), the normal equations lose everything, while QR still gets 8 correct digits.

NoteExample 2: a line fit by QR

Fit a line \(y = c_0 + c_1t\) to the points \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 4)\) by QR.

Solution. The system is \(\M{A}\vect{c} \approx \vect{b}\) with \[ \M{A} = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2}, \qquad \vect{b} = \mat{1 \\ 2 \\ 4}. \] Gram–Schmidt: \(\lVert\vect{a}_1\rVert = \sqrt{3}\), so \(\vect{q}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1, 1, 1)\T\). Then \(r_{12} = \vect{q}_1 \cdot \vect{a}_2 = 3/\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{3}\), and \(\vect{a}_2 - \sqrt{3}\vect{q}_1 = (-1, 0, 1)\T\), of length \(\sqrt{2}\), so \(\vect{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-1, 0, 1)\T\). Hence \[ \M{R} = \mat{\sqrt{3} & \sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2}}, \qquad \M{Q}\T\vect{b} = \mat{7/\sqrt{3} \\ 3/\sqrt{2}}. \] Back substitution: \(\sqrt{2}c_1 = 3/\sqrt{2}\) gives \(c_1 = 1.5\), and \(\sqrt{3}c_0 + \sqrt{3}(1.5) = 7/\sqrt{3}\) gives \(c_0 = \tfrac{7}{3} - 1.5 = \tfrac{5}{6}\). The line is \(y = \tfrac{5}{6} + 1.5t\), the same as from the normal equations, \(3c_0 + 3c_1 = 7\) and \(3c_0 + 5c_1 = 10\). (Software may return \(-\vect{q}_1\) and \(-\vect{q}_2\), with the rows of \(\M{R}\) negated, which gives the same answer.)

NoteExample 3: why the normal equations lose digits

In the table at the start of the chapter, explain the errors for degree 10 using condition numbers.

Solution. \(\kappa(\M{A}) = 2 \times 10^{7}\), so \(\kappa(\M{A}\T\M{A}) = (2 \times 10^7)^2 = 4 \times 10^{14}\), matching the table. By the rule of thumb, the normal equations should lose about 14 of 16 digits, leaving about 2: the observed error, \(2 \times 10^{-3}\), fits. QR should lose about 7, leaving about 9: the observed error, \(10^{-9}\), fits too. The data fit the polynomial exactly here; with noisy data the comparison is less extreme but goes the same way.

Rank-deficient problems and the SVD

If the columns of \(\M{A}\) are dependent, or nearly so, \(\M{R}\) has a zero, or tiny, diagonal entry, and back substitution fails. Two tools handle this:

  • QR with column pivoting chooses, at each step, the remaining column with the largest component outside the span of those already chosen. Its \(\M{R}\) has decreasing diagonal entries, and small ones reveal the numerical rank.
  • The SVD (Chapter 21) is the most reliable: the pseudoinverse gives the shortest least-squares solution, and the singular values show exactly which directions are poorly determined. NumPy’s lstsq uses it.

Libraries compute the SVD in two stages. Householder reflections, applied alternately on the left and the right, reduce \(\M{A}\) to a bidiagonal matrix, with non-zeros only on the diagonal and just above it. Then a variant of the QR algorithm (Chapter 29), or a divide-and-conquer method, finds its singular values. Like everything in this chapter, every step is orthogonal and so backward stable.

30.3 In code

np.linalg.qr(A) returns the reduced factorisation by default, and MATLAB’s qr(A, 0) (or qr(A, "econ")) does the same. For least squares, use np.linalg.lstsq (which uses the SVD), scipy.linalg.lstsq, or MATLAB’s backslash A \ b, which uses Householder QR for a non-square \(\M{A}\).

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

t = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)
for deg in [5, 8, 10, 12]:
    A = np.vander(t, deg + 1, increasing=True)    # columns 1, t, t^2, ...
    c = np.ones(deg + 1)
    b = A @ c
    c_normal = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b)
    Q, R = np.linalg.qr(A)
    c_qr = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ b)            # R is triangular
    print(f"degree {deg:2d}: cond(A) = {np.linalg.cond(A):.1e}, "
          f"error: normal {np.max(abs(c_normal - c)):.1e}, QR {np.max(abs(c_qr - c)):.1e}")

degree  8: cond(A) = 6.3e+05, error: normal 7.2e-06, QR 7.0e-11
degree 10: cond(A) = 2.0e+07, error: normal 2.1e-03, QR 1.6e-10
degree 12: cond(A) = 6.8e+08, error: normal 2.4e+01, QR 2.2e-08
t = linspace(0, 1, 50)';
for deg = [5 8 10 12]
    A = t .^ (0:deg);                 % columns 1, t, t^2, ...
    c = ones(deg + 1, 1);
    b = A * c;
    c_normal = (A' * A) \ (A' * b);
    [Q, R] = qr(A, 0);
    c_qr = R \ (Q' * b);              % R is triangular
    fprintf('degree %2d: cond(A) = %.1e, error: normal %.1e, QR %.1e\n', ...
            deg, cond(A), max(abs(c_normal - c)), max(abs(c_qr - c)));
end

The exact errors vary between libraries and computers, but the pattern doesn’t: the normal equations’ errors grow like \(\kappa(\M{A})^2\varepsilon\), and QR’s like \(\kappa(\M{A})\varepsilon\).

30.4 Check your understanding

TipWhat is \(\M{Q}\T\M{Q}\) for the reduced QR factorisation? What about \(\M{Q}\M{Q}\T\)?

\(\M{Q}\T\M{Q} = \M{I}\) (\(n \times n\)), because the columns are orthonormal. \(\M{Q}\M{Q}\T\) (\(m \times m\)) is not the identity when \(m > n\): it is the projection onto the column space of \(\M{A}\) (Chapter 17).

TipIf \(\kappa(\M{A}) = 10^6\), about how many digits do the normal equations and QR lose?

About 12 and about 6.

TipWhy is a Householder reflection its own inverse?

Reflecting twice in the same mirror returns every vector to where it started. Algebraically, \(\M{H}\) is symmetric and orthogonal, so \(\M{H}^{-1} = \M{H}\T = \M{H}\).

30.5 Key ideas

  • Every matrix with independent columns can be factorised as \(\M{A} = \M{Q}\M{R}\), with orthonormal columns in \(\M{Q}\) and \(\M{R}\) upper triangular.
  • Gram–Schmidt gives \(\M{Q}\) directly, but loses orthogonality in floating point; Householder reflections are stable.
  • Least squares with QR solves \(\M{R}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{Q}\T\vect{b}\).
  • The normal equations square the condition number, so they lose about twice as many digits as QR.
  • For rank-deficient problems, the SVD gives the most reliable answer.

30.7 In practice

  • Curve fitting: numpy.polyfit and MATLAB’s polyfit use QR. For high degrees, centring and scaling \(t\) to \([-1, 1]\), or using orthogonal polynomials such as Chebyshev polynomials as the basis, improves the conditioning of \(\M{A}\) itself.
  • Statistics: regression software computes coefficients by QR. Its \(\M{R}\) also gives the standard errors cheaply, via \((\M{A}\T\M{A})^{-1} = \M{R}^{-1}\M{R}^{-\mathsf{T}}\), without ever forming \(\M{A}\T\M{A}\).
  • Signal processing and navigation: Kalman filters and adaptive filters update least-squares solutions as each new measurement arrives, using QR updates that stay accurate over millions of steps.
  • Eigenvalues: QR factorisations are the engine of the QR algorithm (Chapter 29).

30.8 Going further

  • Proofs that Householder reflections are orthogonal and send \(\vect{x}\) to a multiple of \(\ue{1}\), and that \(\kappa(\M{A}\T\M{A}) = \kappa(\M{A})^2\): Section C.21.
  • Givens rotations zero one entry at a time, by rotating in the plane of two coordinates. They are ideal for sparse matrices and for updating a factorisation when a row is added.
  • Rank-revealing QR, QR with column pivoting, is a cheaper alternative to the SVD for finding the numerical rank and a basis for the column space.
  • Björck’s Numerical Methods for Least Squares Problems covers all of this, and much more, in depth.
