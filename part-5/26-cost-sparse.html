26  Cost, banded and sparse systems

Further

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • count the arithmetic operations in matrix calculations, and predict running times
  • solve tridiagonal systems with the Thomas algorithm, in time proportional to \(n\)
  • store sparse matrices efficiently, and recognise fill-in
  • choose between dense, banded and sparse solvers

Builds on Chapter 11, Chapter 13.

Engineering and scientific models routinely lead to linear systems with a million unknowns: one for each point of a grid covering a wing, a reservoir or a weather system. Gaussian elimination on a full \(10^6 \times 10^6\) matrix would need about \(7 \times 10^{17}\) operations and 8 terabytes of memory. Yet such systems are solved every day, on ordinary computers, in seconds. The reason is that their matrices are sparse: almost all their entries are zero, because each grid point is connected only to its few neighbours. This chapter shows how to count the cost of a calculation, and how to exploit zeros so that most of that cost disappears.

26.1 Build intuition

There is no app for this chapter. Instead, consider the two matrices in Figure 26.1. Both are \(12 \times 12\), with non-zero entries only on the diagonal and in one row and one column, and in fact they are the same system with the unknowns numbered in the opposite order. Before reading the caption, predict: when you carry out elimination on each, which entries that start as zero will become non-zero?

Show the code for this figure 
import numpy as np
import scipy.linalg
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

n = 12
A = np.eye(n) * 4
A[0, :] = A[:, 0] = 1
A[0, 0] = 4
B = A[::-1, ::-1]                       # number the unknowns in reverse

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(10, 2.8))
for ax, M, title in [(axes[0], A, "A"), (axes[2], B, "A, reordered")]:
    ax.spy(M, markersize=4, color="#2153b8")

for ax, M in [(axes[1], A), (axes[3], B)]:

    LU = np.abs(L) + np.abs(U) > 1e-12
    ax.spy(LU, markersize=4, color="#9c5208")
    ax.set_title(f"L and U: {LU.sum()} non-zeros", fontsize=10)

26.2 The ideas

Counting operations

The running time of a numerical method is roughly proportional to the number of arithmetic operations it performs, its flops (floating-point operations). Counting them for the basic tasks:

Task Operations (approximately)
Dot product of two vectors of length \(n\) \(2n\)
Matrix–vector product \(\M{A}\vect{x}\), \(\M{A}\) of size \(n \times n\) \(2n^2\)
Matrix–matrix product of two \(n \times n\) matrices \(2n^3\)
Forward or back substitution with a triangular matrix \(n^2\)
LU factorisation (Gaussian elimination) \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\)
Inverse \(\M{A}^{-1}\) \(2n^3\)

Only the leading power of \(n\) matters for large \(n\). An \(n^3\) method gets 8 times slower when \(n\) doubles, and 1000 times slower when \(n\) grows tenfold. A laptop does somewhere around \(10^{10}\) to \(10^{11}\) operations a second, so a dense system with \(n = 1000\) takes a few hundredths of a second, \(n = 10^4\) takes seconds, and \(n = 10^5\) takes hours and needs 80 GB just to store the matrix.

Two practical rules follow from the table.

  • Factorise once, solve many times. Once \(\M{L}\) and \(\M{U}\) are known, each new right-hand side costs only \(2n^2\). If you need to solve with the same matrix many times, as in a simulation stepping forward in time, keep the factors (Chapter 13).
  • Don’t compute the inverse to solve a system. Computing \(\M{A}^{-1}\) and then \(\M{A}^{-1}\vect{b}\) costs three times as much as elimination, and is less accurate.
NoteExample 1: how long will it take?

A dense system with \(n = 2000\) takes 0.1 seconds to solve on a certain computer. Estimate the time for \(n = 20\,000\), and the memory needed for its matrix.

Solution. The cost grows like \(n^3\), so ten times as many unknowns take \(10^3 = 1000\) times as long: about 100 seconds. The matrix has \(20\,000^2 = 4 \times 10^8\) entries of 8 bytes each, 3.2 GB. Increasing to \(n = 200\,000\) would take about a day, and 320 GB of memory, which is why large problems need sparse methods.

Banded and tridiagonal matrices

A matrix is banded if its non-zero entries all lie within a distance \(p\) of the main diagonal: \(a_{ij} = 0\) whenever \(\lvert i - j\rvert > p\). Eliminating below a pivot then involves only the \(p\) rows below it, and only the \(p\) entries to its right, so elimination without row swaps never creates non-zeros outside the band. The cost drops from \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) to about \(2np^2\).

The most important case is the tridiagonal matrix, with \(p = 1:\) \[ \mat{d_1 & c_1 & & & \\ a_2 & d_2 & c_2 & & \\ & a_3 & d_3 & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & \ddots & c_{n-1} \\ & & & a_n & d_n}. \] Tridiagonal systems come from anything arranged in a line, where each point interacts only with its two neighbours: heat flow along a rod, a hanging cable, a chain of masses and springs, spline curves. Elimination simplifies to the Thomas algorithm: one sweep down, removing each \(a_i\), and one sweep up, back substitution.

ImportantThe Thomas algorithm

Forward sweep. Set \(c'_1 = c_1/d_1\) and \(b'_1 = b_1/d_1\). For \(i = 2, \dots, n\): \[ m = d_i - a_ic'_{i-1}, \qquad c'_i = c_i/m, \qquad b'_i = (b_i - a_ib'_{i-1})/m. \] Back substitution. Set \(x_n = b'_n\). For \(i = n-1, \dots, 1\): \(x_i = b'_i - c'_ix_{i+1}\).

It needs about \(8n\) operations instead of \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\): a million unknowns in a few milliseconds. It uses no pivoting, which is safe when the matrix is diagonally dominant or symmetric positive definite (Chapter 25), as it is in most applications.

NoteExample 2: the Thomas algorithm

Solve \[ \mat{2 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 2}\vect{x} = \mat{1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1}. \]

Solution. Here every \(d_i = 2\), \(a_i = -1\) and \(c_i = -1\). The forward sweep gives \[ \begin{aligned} c'_1 &= -\tfrac{1}{2}, & b'_1 &= \tfrac{1}{2}, \\ m &= 2 - \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{3}{2}, & c'_2 &= -\tfrac{2}{3}, & b'_2 &= \bigl(0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\big/\tfrac{3}{2} = \tfrac{1}{3}, \\ m &= 2 - \tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{4}{3}, & c'_3 &= -\tfrac{3}{4}, & b'_3 &= \bigl(0 + \tfrac{1}{3}\bigr)\big/\tfrac{4}{3} = \tfrac{1}{4}, \\ m &= 2 - \tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{5}{4}, & & & b'_4 &= \bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{4}\bigr)\big/\tfrac{5}{4} = 1. \end{aligned} \] Back substitution: \(x_4 = 1\), \(x_3 = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 1 = 1\), \(x_2 = \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 1 = 1\), \(x_1 = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = 1\). So \(\vect{x} = (1, 1, 1, 1)\T\).

Sparse matrices

A matrix is sparse when most of its entries are zero and it pays to take advantage of that. A grid of \(1000 \times 1000\) points with each point linked to its four neighbours gives a \(10^6 \times 10^6\) matrix with about \(5 \times 10^6\) non-zeros: five per row. Storing all \(10^{12}\) entries would be absurd. Instead, sparse formats store only the non-zeros and their positions. The most common, compressed sparse row (CSR), uses three lists:

  • the non-zero values, row by row;
  • the column number of each value;
  • for each row, where its values start in the first list (with one extra entry at the end).

For example, \[ \M{A} = \mat{5 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 & 7} \quad\text{is stored as}\quad \begin{aligned} \text{values} &= (5, 1, 3, 2, 4, 6, 7), \\ \text{columns} &= (0, 3, 1, 0, 2, 2, 3), \\ \text{row starts} &= (0, 2, 3, 5, 7), \end{aligned} \] counting from 0, as Python does. A matrix–vector product then needs only 2 operations per non-zero.

NoteExample 3: storage for a grid problem

Heat flow on a square plate is modelled on a grid of \(500 \times 500\) interior points, each linked to its four neighbours (Figure 26.2 shows a small version). Compare the storage needed for the matrix in dense and sparse form.

Solution. There are \(n = 250\,000\) unknowns. Dense storage needs \(n^2 = 6.25 \times 10^{10}\) numbers, 500 GB. Each row has at most 5 non-zeros, so CSR stores at most \(1.25 \times 10^6\) values and \(1.25 \times 10^6\) column numbers, plus \(n + 1\) row starts: about 20 MB, 25 000 times less.

Fill-in and reordering

Elimination on a sparse matrix can create new non-zeros where there were zeros, called fill-in. Subtracting a multiple of the pivot row puts non-zeros wherever the pivot row has them. In the arrow matrix of Figure 26.1, the first row is full, so eliminating with it fills in the whole matrix, and the factors are dense. Numbering the unknowns in the reverse order puts the full row last, and there is no fill-in at all.

The order of the unknowns doesn’t change the solution, but it can change the cost of elimination enormously. Sparse direct solvers, such as SciPy’s spsolve and MATLAB’s backslash on a sparse matrix, first reorder the unknowns with fast heuristics (such as approximate minimum degree or nested dissection) designed to keep fill-in small, and only then factorise.

When even the reordered factors are too large, which happens for big three-dimensional problems, iterative methods take over. They need only matrix–vector products, so they never create fill-in (Chapter 27).

26.3 In code

Run this chapter’s code yourself: open the notebook in Colab, or download the Jupyter notebook or the MATLAB script.

SciPy’s scipy.sparse module stores sparse matrices, and scipy.sparse.linalg.spsolve solves sparse systems. In MATLAB, sparse and spdiags create sparse matrices, and backslash automatically uses a sparse solver for them. The example solves \(-u'' = 1\) on \(0 < t < 1\), with \(u = 0\) at both ends, by finite differences (Chapter 33): \(n\) grid points give a tridiagonal system. Its exact solution is \(u = t(1 - t)/2\).

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp
from scipy.sparse.linalg import spsolve
import time

def thomas(a, d, c, b):
    """Solve a tridiagonal system: a below, d on and c above the diagonal."""
    n = len(d)
    cp, bp = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n)
    cp[0], bp[0] = c[0] / d[0], b[0] / d[0]
    for i in range(1, n):
        m = d[i] - a[i] * cp[i - 1]
        cp[i] = c[i] / m if i < n - 1 else 0
        bp[i] = (b[i] - a[i] * bp[i - 1]) / m
    x = np.zeros(n)
    x[-1] = bp[-1]
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        x[i] = bp[i] - cp[i] * x[i + 1]
    return x

print(thomas([0, -1, -1, -1], [2, 2, 2, 2], [-1, -1, -1, 0], [1, 0, 0, 1]))

n = 100_000
h = 1 / (n + 1)
A = sp.diags([-1.0, 2.0, -1.0], [-1, 0, 1], shape=(n, n), format="csr")
b = h**2 * np.ones(n)
start = time.perf_counter()
u = spsolve(A, b)
print(f"sparse solve: {time.perf_counter() - start:.3f} s, {A.nnz} non-zeros")
t = np.arange(1, n + 1) * h
print(np.max(np.abs(u - t * (1 - t) / 2)))     # error

26.4 Check your understanding

TipSolving one dense system with \(n = 500\) takes 10 ms. About how long for \(n = 1000\)?

About 80 ms: doubling \(n\) multiplies the \(n^3\) cost by 8.

TipWhy is computing \(\M{A}^{-1}\vect{b}\) a bad way to solve \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\), even for one right-hand side?

It costs about \(2n^3\) operations instead of \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\), and it is less accurate. For a sparse matrix it is far worse: the inverse is usually completely full.

TipHow many operations does a matrix–vector product take for a sparse matrix with \(N\) non-zeros?

About \(2N\): one multiplication and one addition per non-zero, whatever the size of the matrix.

26.5 Key ideas

  • Counting operations predicts running times: dense elimination costs about \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\), so it slows down quickly as \(n\) grows.
  • A tridiagonal system can be solved by the Thomas algorithm in time proportional to \(n\).
  • Sparse matrices are stored by their non-zero entries only, which can save almost all of the memory and the work.
  • Elimination can create new non-zeros (fill-in), and reordering the unknowns can reduce it a great deal.
  • Choose a solver to match the structure: dense for small or full matrices, banded or sparse when most entries are zero.

26.7 In practice

  • Finite differences and finite elements turn differential equations into sparse systems: tridiagonal for problems in a line, banded or more general sparse patterns in two and three dimensions (Chapter 33).
  • Circuit simulation packages, such as SPICE, solve sparse systems with one unknown per node, because each component touches only two nodes (Chapter 32).
  • Networks: the link matrices of road networks, power grids and the web are extremely sparse; the web’s has billions of rows and a few dozen non-zeros in each (Chapter 35).
  • Splines: drawing a smooth cubic curve through \(n\) points needs a tridiagonal solve, which is why spline routines are so fast.
  • GPUs do dense matrix multiplication at enormous speed, so for medium-sized problems a dense method on a GPU can beat a sparse one on a CPU. Counting operations is a guide, not the final word.

26.8 Going further

  • Davis’s Direct Methods for Sparse Linear Systems explains sparse factorisations and the reordering methods behind spsolve and backslash, with code.
  • Strassen’s algorithm multiplies matrices in about \(n^{2.81}\) operations instead of \(2n^3\), by a clever way of combining \(2 \times 2\) blocks. Methods with even lower powers exist, but only win for impractically large \(n\).
  • Modern computers are often limited by moving data rather than by arithmetic, so libraries arrange calculations in blocks that fit in fast memory. This is why a library routine can be 100 times faster than the same operation count in a simple loop.
