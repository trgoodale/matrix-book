29  Eigenvalue algorithms

Further

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • find the dominant eigenvalue and eigenvector with the power method
  • use inverse iteration and shifts to find any eigenvalue you choose
  • estimate eigenvalues with the Rayleigh quotient
  • outline the QR algorithm that eig uses

Builds on Chapter 13, Chapter 18, Chapter 19.

In Chapter 18, eigenvalues were found as the roots of the characteristic polynomial. That works by hand for \(2 \times 2\) and \(3 \times 3\) matrices, but it is useless on a computer: there is no formula for the roots of polynomials of degree 5 or more, and the roots are extremely sensitive to rounding errors in the coefficients. Software instead finds eigenvalues by iteration, improving approximations to eigenvectors step by step. This chapter describes the simplest such method, the power method, its powerful variants, and the QR algorithm that runs inside eig in every numerical library.

29.2 The ideas

The power method

Chapter 19 showed that multiplying a vector by \(\M{A}\) over and over brings it into line with the eigenvector of the dominant eigenvalue, the one largest in size. Writing \(\vect{x}_0 = c_1\vect{v}_1 + \dots + c_n\vect{v}_n\) in terms of the eigenvectors, \[ \M{A}^k\vect{x}_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k\vect{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\vect{v}_2 + \dots = \lambda_1^k\left(c_1\vect{v}_1 + c_2\Bigl(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\Bigr)^k\vect{v}_2 + \cdots\right), \] and if \(\lvert\lambda_1\rvert > \lvert\lambda_2\rvert \ge \dots\), all the terms but the first die away. The vector itself grows or shrinks like \(\lambda_1^k\), so to stop it overflowing, the power method rescales it to length 1 at every step:

ImportantThe power method

Start with any vector \(\vect{q}_0\) of length 1. Repeat: \[ \vect{y} = \M{A}\vect{q}_k, \qquad \vect{q}_{k+1} = \vect{y}/\lVert\vect{y}\rVert. \] \(\vect{q}_k\) approaches the dominant eigenvector (up to sign), and the error shrinks by a factor of about \(\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert\) per step.

Each step costs one matrix–vector product, cheap even for huge sparse matrices. The weakness is speed: if \(\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert = 0.99\), gaining each digit takes about 230 steps. The method also fails if \(c_1 = 0\) (in practice, rounding errors soon introduce some \(\vect{v}_1\)), and if two eigenvalues share the largest size, as with \(\pm\lambda\) or a complex pair.

NoteExample 1: the power method by hand

Apply the power method to \(\M{A} = \mat{2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3}\), starting from \(\vect{q}_0 = (1, 0)\T\), and estimate the dominant eigenvalue with the Rayleigh quotient.

Solution. The eigenvalues are \((5 \pm \sqrt{5})/2\), about 3.618 and 1.382. \[ \begin{aligned} \M{A}\vect{q}_0 &= (2, 1)\T, & \vect{q}_1 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(2, 1)\T, & r &= \tfrac{1}{5}(2, 1)\mat{5 \\ 5} = 3, \\ \M{A}\vect{q}_1 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(5, 5)\T, & \vect{q}_2 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)\T, & r &= \tfrac{1}{2}(1, 1)\mat{3 \\ 4} = 3.5, \\ \M{A}\vect{q}_2 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(3, 4)\T, & \vect{q}_3 &= (0.6, 0.8)\T, & r &= (0.6, 0.8)\mat{2 \\ 3} = 3.6. \end{aligned} \] The next estimates are 3.6154 and 3.6176. The errors in the estimates, 0.618, 0.118, 0.018, 0.0026, shrink by a factor of about 0.146 each step. That is \((1.382/3.618)^2\): the square of the factor for the vectors, because \(\M{A}\) is symmetric.

The Rayleigh quotient

Given an approximate eigenvector \(\vect{q}\) of length 1, the best estimate of its eigenvalue is the Rayleigh quotient \[ r(\vect{q}) = \vect{q}\T\M{A}\vect{q}, \] the number \(\lambda\) that makes \(\M{A}\vect{q} - \lambda\vect{q}\) as short as possible. If \(\vect{q}\) is an exact eigenvector, \(r(\vect{q}) = \lambda\). For a symmetric matrix, the Rayleigh quotient is especially accurate: if \(\vect{q}\) is wrong by a small amount \(\delta\), \(r(\vect{q})\) is wrong only by an amount proportional to \(\delta^2\). So the eigenvalue estimates converge twice as fast as the vectors, gaining digits at twice the rate.

Inverse iteration and shifts

The eigenvalues of \(\M{A}^{-1}\) are \(1/\lambda_i\), with the same eigenvectors (Chapter 18, exercise 4). So the power method applied to \(\M{A}^{-1}\) finds the eigenvalue of \(\M{A}\) smallest in size. This is inverse iteration. Each step needs \(\vect{y} = \M{A}^{-1}\vect{q}_k\), but, as always, don’t form the inverse: factorise \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\) once, and solve \(\M{A}\vect{y} = \vect{q}_k\) with the factors at each step (Chapter 13).

A shift makes this far more useful. The eigenvalues of \(\M{A} - \mu\M{I}\) are \(\lambda_i - \mu\), so inverse iteration with \(\M{A} - \mu\M{I}\) finds the eigenvalue of \(\M{A}\) closest to \(\mu\). The error factor per step is \[ \frac{\lvert\lambda_{\text{closest}} - \mu\rvert}{\lvert\lambda_{\text{next closest}} - \mu\rvert}, \] which is tiny when \(\mu\) is a good guess. Shifted inverse iteration can find any eigenvalue you have a rough estimate of, and is the standard way to find an eigenvector once its eigenvalue is known accurately.

To see it, go back to the app at the start of the chapter and choose Equal sizes, where the power method never settles. Then choose Shifted inverse and move the shift. Which eigenvalue does it find for each shift, and how fast? What happens as the shift gets close to an eigenvalue?

Taking the shift to be the current Rayleigh quotient at every step gives Rayleigh quotient iteration, which, for symmetric matrices, converges cubically: the number of correct digits roughly triples at each step. The price is a new factorisation at every step.

NoteExample 2: shifted inverse iteration

Find the smaller eigenvalue of the matrix in Example 1 by inverse iteration with shift \(\mu = 1\), starting from \((1, 0)\T\).

Solution. \(\M{A} - \M{I} = \mat{1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2}\), whose inverse is \(\mat{2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1}\). (For a \(2 \times 2\) example it is fine to write the inverse down; for a large matrix, use its LU factors.) Then \[ \vect{y} = (2, -1)\T, \qquad \vect{q}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(2, -1)\T, \qquad r = \tfrac{1}{5}(2, -1)\mat{3 \\ -1} = 1.4. \] The next estimates are 1.38235, 1.381974 and 1.3819662, converging to \(1.3819660\ldots\) with the error shrinking by a factor of about 0.02 per step. The vector factor is \(\lvert 1.382 - 1\rvert/\lvert 3.618 - 1\rvert \approx 0.146\), and the estimates gain at its square, as in Example 1.

The QR algorithm

To find all the eigenvalues of a dense matrix, libraries use the QR algorithm. Factorise \(\M{A}_0 = \M{A}\) as \(\M{A}_0 = \M{Q}_0\M{R}_0\), with \(\M{Q}_0\) orthogonal and \(\M{R}_0\) upper triangular (introduced in Chapter 17; Chapter 30 shows how to compute it), then multiply the factors in the opposite order, and repeat: \[ \M{A}_k = \M{Q}_k\M{R}_k, \qquad \M{A}_{k+1} = \M{R}_k\M{Q}_k. \] Since \(\M{R}_k = \M{Q}_k\T\M{A}_k\), each new matrix is \(\M{A}_{k+1} = \M{Q}_k\T\M{A}_k\M{Q}_k\): a change of basis by an orthogonal matrix (Chapter 15), so it has the same eigenvalues as \(\M{A}\). Remarkably, the \(\M{A}_k\) converge to an upper triangular matrix (diagonal, if \(\M{A}\) is symmetric), with the eigenvalues on its diagonal. In effect the QR algorithm runs the power method on many vectors at once, keeping them orthogonal, so that each settles onto a different eigenvector.

The basic version converges at the same slow rate as the power method. Production versions, in LAPACK and so in NumPy and MATLAB, add three refinements that make it very fast:

  1. first reduce \(\M{A}\) to Hessenberg form (zero below the first subdiagonal; tridiagonal if \(\M{A}\) is symmetric) by orthogonal changes of basis, so that each QR step costs \(O(n^2)\) instead of \(O(n^3)\);
  2. use shifts, like inverse iteration, so that the last diagonal entry converges in a few steps;
  3. once it has converged, deflate: remove the last row and column and carry on with a smaller matrix.

The total cost is about \(10n^3\) operations for all the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a dense matrix, and the computed eigenvalues are exact for a matrix within rounding error of \(\M{A}\).

NoteExample 3: the QR algorithm

Apply the basic QR algorithm to the matrix of Example 1.

Solution. The first steps give (see the code below) \[ \M{A}_1 = \mat{3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2}, \quad \M{A}_2 = \mat{3.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1.5}, \quad \M{A}_3 = \mat{3.6 & -0.2 \\ -0.2 & 1.4}, \quad \M{A}_5 \approx \mat{3.6176 & -0.0294 \\ -0.0294 & 1.3824}. \] Every \(\M{A}_k\) is symmetric with trace 5 and determinant 5, as it must be, since they all have the same eigenvalues. The off-diagonal entries shrink by a factor of about \(1.382/3.618 \approx 0.38\) per step, and the diagonal entries approach the eigenvalues. The top-left entries, 3, 3.5, 3.6, …, are exactly the Rayleigh quotients from the power method in Example 1: the QR algorithm really is the power method in disguise.

29.3 In code

For large sparse matrices, when only a few eigenvalues are needed, use scipy.sparse.linalg.eigs (or eigsh for symmetric matrices) in Python and eigs in MATLAB. They use Krylov methods, Lanczos and Arnoldi, which extract far more from the sequence \(\vect{q}, \M{A}\vect{q}, \M{A}^2\vect{q}, \dots\) than the power method does, and with the option sigma they use shifted inverse iteration to find the eigenvalues nearest a given value.

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np
import scipy.linalg

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 3.0]])

# Power method
q = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
for k in range(1, 6):
    y = A @ q
    q = y / np.linalg.norm(y)
    print(f"{k}  {q @ A @ q:.10f}")          # Rayleigh quotient

# Shifted inverse iteration: factorise once, solve at each step
lu = scipy.linalg.lu_factor(A - 1.0 * np.eye(2))
q = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
for k in range(1, 5):
    y = scipy.linalg.lu_solve(lu, q)
    q = y / np.linalg.norm(y)
    print(f"{k}  {q @ A @ q:.10f}")

# Basic QR algorithm
Ak = A.copy()
for k in range(5):
    Q, R = np.linalg.qr(Ak)
    Ak = R @ Q
print(np.round(Ak, 4))

29.4 Check your understanding

TipA matrix has eigenvalues 10, 9 and 1. Will the power method converge quickly?

No: the factor per step is \(9/10 = 0.9\), so each correct digit takes about 22 steps. A shift would help: the power method on \(\M{A} - 5\M{I}\), with eigenvalues 5, 4 and \(-4\), has factor \(0.8\); better still, inverse iteration with a shift near 10.

TipWhat does inverse iteration with shift \(\mu\) find if \(\mu\) is exactly an eigenvalue?

In exact arithmetic \(\M{A} - \mu\M{I}\) is singular and can’t be factorised. In floating point, it is nearly singular, and solving with it gives an enormous vector pointing almost exactly along the eigenvector: one step is enough.

TipWhy does eig not use the characteristic polynomial?

Its roots can be extremely sensitive to small changes in its coefficients, so rounding errors would ruin them; and computing the coefficients is itself expensive and unstable. The QR algorithm works on the matrix directly, with orthogonal transformations that don’t magnify errors.

29.5 Key ideas

  • The power method multiplies a vector by \(\M{A}\) over and over, and converges to the dominant eigenvector at a rate set by \(\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert\).
  • The Rayleigh quotient \(\vect{x}\T\M{A}\vect{x}/\vect{x}\T\vect{x}\) turns an approximate eigenvector into an accurate eigenvalue.
  • Inverse iteration with a shift \(\sigma\) finds the eigenvalue closest to \(\sigma\), and converges very fast when \(\sigma\) is close.
  • The QR algorithm, which eig uses, finds all the eigenvalues together by repeated QR factorisations.

29.7 In practice

  • PageRank: Google’s original ranking of web pages is the dominant eigenvector of a huge matrix of links, found by the power method, which needs only one sparse matrix–vector product per step (Chapter 35).
  • Vibration and buckling: engineers need the few lowest natural frequencies of a structure, or the lowest buckling load, which are the smallest eigenvalues of huge sparse stiffness matrices. Shifted inverse iteration and Lanczos methods find them without touching the rest.
  • Quantum chemistry and physics: the energy levels of molecules and materials are eigenvalues of very large symmetric matrices, found by Lanczos and Davidson methods.
  • Principal component analysis of large data sets needs only the first few principal components, which randomised and Krylov methods find quickly (Chapter 21).
  • Stability checks: the stability of a power grid or an aircraft depends on the eigenvalue of a large matrix with the largest real part (Chapter 23), which Arnoldi’s method finds without computing the others.

29.8 Going further

  • A proof that the power method converges, at the rate claimed: Section C.20.
  • The Lanczos method (for symmetric matrices) and the Arnoldi method (for general ones) build an orthonormal basis for the vectors \(\vect{q}, \M{A}\vect{q}, \dots, \M{A}^{m-1}\vect{q}\), and find the eigenvalues of a small \(m \times m\) matrix that represents \(\M{A}\) in that basis. Its extreme eigenvalues approach those of \(\M{A}\) very quickly. These are what eigs uses.
  • Trefethen and Bau’s Numerical Linear Algebra explains the QR algorithm, and why shifts make it converge so fast, very clearly. Golub and Van Loan’s Matrix Computations is the encyclopaedia.
