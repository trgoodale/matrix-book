25  Pivoting

Further

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • explain why elimination without row swaps can fail, or give wildly wrong answers
  • apply partial pivoting, and write the result as \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\)
  • recognise when scaled pivoting is needed, and when no pivoting is needed at all

Builds on Chapter 11, Chapter 13, Chapter 24.

Gaussian elimination, as described in Chapter 11, swaps rows only when a pivot is exactly zero. On a computer that is not enough. A pivot that is merely small produces huge multipliers, which swamp the other numbers in the matrix, and the rounding errors that follow can destroy the answer, even for a perfectly well-conditioned system. The cure is cheap: before each elimination step, swap rows so that the pivot is as large as possible. This is partial pivoting, and every library solver does it.

25.1 Build intuition

The app carries out elimination on a pretend computer that keeps only a few significant digits, rounding after every operation, so that the effects of rounding are large enough to see. Real computers keep about 16 digits, and the same things happen to larger systems.

  • Increase the number of digits kept, with no pivoting. Does it fix the problem, or only make it smaller?
  • Three equations uses 4 digits. Compare the multipliers with and without pivoting.

25.2 The ideas

When elimination without swaps goes wrong

Consider \[ \begin{aligned} 10^{-20}x + y &= 1, \\ x + y &= 2, \end{aligned} \] whose solution is extremely close to \(x = 1\), \(y = 1\). The matrix is well conditioned (\(\kappa \approx 2.6\)). Eliminating without a swap uses the pivot \(10^{-20}\) and the multiplier \(10^{20}\): \[ (1 - 10^{20})\,y = 2 - 10^{20}. \] In double precision, \(1 - 10^{20}\) and \(2 - 10^{20}\) both round to \(-10^{20}\): the 1 and the 2 are far below the 16 digits kept. So \(y = 1\), and then \(10^{-20}x = 1 - y = 0\) gives \(x = 0\). The second equation has been completely swamped by a huge multiple of the first.

Swapping the equations first uses the pivot 1 and the multiplier \(10^{-20}\): \[ (1 - 10^{-20})\,y = 1 - 2 \times 10^{-20}, \] which rounds to \(y = 1\), and then \(x = 2 - y = 1\). Both answers are correct to 16 digits.

The general lesson: a large multiplier magnifies the rounding errors in the pivot row, and lets them swamp the other rows. Small multipliers keep errors small.

Partial pivoting

ImportantPartial pivoting

At each step \(k\) of elimination, look at the entries in column \(k\) on and below the diagonal. Swap the row containing the largest in size into the pivot position, then eliminate as usual.

The pivot is then at least as large as every entry below it, so every multiplier is at most 1 in size. This doesn’t remove rounding errors, but it stops them from being magnified at each step.

All the row swaps can be collected into a permutation matrix \(\M{P}\), as in Chapter 13, giving \[ \M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}, \] with every entry of \(\M{L}\) at most 1 in size. This is what scipy.linalg.lu, MATLAB’s lu, and the solvers behind np.linalg.solve and MATLAB’s backslash compute. The extra cost is tiny: searching a column takes \(n\) comparisons, while eliminating with it takes about \(n^2\) arithmetic operations.

NoteExample 1: four-digit arithmetic, with and without pivoting

Solve \[ \begin{aligned} 0.0003x + 1.566y &= 1.569, \\ 0.3454x - 2.436y &= 1.018 \end{aligned} \] rounding every result to 4 significant digits, first without pivoting and then with partial pivoting. The exact solution is \(x = 10\), \(y = 1\).

Solution. Without pivoting, the multiplier is \(0.3454/0.0003 = 1151.3\ldots\), which rounds to \(1151\). Then, rounding each product and each difference to 4 digits, \[ \begin{aligned} a_{22} &= -2.436 - 1151 \times 1.566 \\ &= -2.436 - 1802 = -1804, \\ b_2 &= 1.018 - 1151 \times 1.569 \\ &= 1.018 - 1806 = -1805. \end{aligned} \] So \(y = 1805/1804 = 1.001\), and \[ x = \frac{1.569 - 1.566 \times 1.001}{0.0003} = \frac{1.569 - 1.568}{0.0003} = 3.333. \] The small error in \(y\) was divided by the tiny pivot \(0.0003\), giving an answer for \(x\) that is 67% wrong.

With partial pivoting, \(0.3454 > 0.0003\), so swap the equations. The multiplier is \(0.0003/0.3454 = 0.0008686\), and \[ a_{22} = 1.566 + 0.0008686 \times 2.436 = 1.568, \qquad b_2 = 1.569 - 0.0008686 \times 1.018 = 1.568, \] so \(y = 1.000\) and \(x = (1.018 + 2.436 \times 1.000)/0.3454 = 10.00\), correct to all 4 digits.

NoteExample 2: \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\) with partial pivoting

Factorise \(\M{A} = \mat{1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 6 & 1 \\ 4 & 8 & 6}\) with partial pivoting, and use the factors to solve \(\M{A}\vect{x} = (4, 9, 18)\T\).

Solution. In column 1 the largest entry is 4, in row 3, so swap rows 1 and 3. The multipliers are \(\ell_{21} = 2/4 = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\ell_{31} = 1/4\): \[ \mat{4 & 8 & 6 \\ 2 & 6 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1} \;\to\; \mat{4 & 8 & 6 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -\tfrac{1}{2}}. \] In column 2 the candidates are 2 and 0, so no swap is needed, and the multiplier is 0. So \[ \M{P} = \mat{0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0}, \qquad \M{L} = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ \tfrac{1}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \tfrac{1}{4} & 0 & 1}, \qquad \M{U} = \mat{4 & 8 & 6 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & -\tfrac{1}{2}}. \] Every multiplier is at most 1 in size, as promised. To solve, reorder \(\vect{b}\) in the same way, \(\M{P}\vect{b} = (18, 9, 4)\T\). Forward substitution with \(\M{L}\) gives \(\vect{y} = (18, 0, -\tfrac{1}{2})\T\), and back substitution with \(\M{U}\) gives \(\vect{x} = (1, 1, 1)\T\).

How well does it work?

Errors can still grow if the entries of the matrix grow during elimination. The growth factor is the largest entry that appears in \(\M{U}\) divided by the largest entry of \(\M{A}\). With partial pivoting it can be as large as \(2^{n-1}\): Wilkinson’s example, with 1s on the diagonal and in the last column and \(-1\)s below the diagonal, achieves it (exercise 4). In practice such matrices essentially never arise: decades of experience show growth factors stay small for real problems, which is why partial pivoting is trusted everywhere.

Scaled and complete pivoting

Partial pivoting compares the sizes of entries in one column, but that comparison can mislead when the rows are on very different scales. Multiplying an equation by \(10^6\) makes its entries look large without changing the system. Two refinements deal with this:

  • Scaled partial pivoting compares each candidate pivot with the largest entry in its own row, and picks the row where the pivot is largest relative to the rest of its row.
  • Complete pivoting searches the whole remaining submatrix for the largest entry and swaps both rows and columns to bring it into the pivot position. Its growth factor is much better bounded, but the search costs about \(n^3/3\) comparisons, so it is rarely used.

To see this, go back to the app at the start of the chapter and choose Badly scaled. Partial pivoting doesn’t help: look at the sizes of the numbers in each row, and then try Scaled partial.

A simpler and very effective alternative to scaled pivoting is to equilibrate the system first, dividing each row by its largest entry, so that all rows have similar sizes.

NoteExample 3: when partial pivoting is fooled by scaling

In 4-digit arithmetic, solve \[ \begin{aligned} 30x + 591\,400y &= 591\,700, \\ 5.291x - 6.130y &= 46.78, \end{aligned} \] whose exact solution is \(x = 10\), \(y = 1\).

Solution. This is Example 1 with the first equation multiplied by \(10^5\). Partial pivoting compares 30 with 5.291 and makes no swap, so it repeats the mistake of Example 1: the 4-digit result is \(x = -10.00\), \(y = 1.001\). The pivot 30 looked large, but it is tiny compared with the rest of its row.

Scaled partial pivoting compares \(30/591\,400 \approx 0.00005\) with \(5.291/6.130 \approx 0.86\), swaps the equations, and gets \(x = 10.00\), \(y = 1.000\). This is Badly scaled in the app.

When pivoting isn’t needed

Two important kinds of matrix are safe without any row swaps:

  • Symmetric positive definite matrices (Chapter 20). Elimination never meets a small pivot, and the Cholesky factorisation \(\M{L}\M{L}\T\) needs no pivoting.
  • Diagonally dominant matrices, where each diagonal entry is larger in size than the sum of the other entries in its column. Partial pivoting would make no swaps, so it can be skipped, which matters for the banded systems of Chapter 26: swapping rows can widen the band.

25.3 In code

Adding partial pivoting to the elimination function from Chapter 11 takes three lines: find the row with the largest entry in the pivot column, and swap it into place, in both \(\M{A}\) and \(\vect{b}\).

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np
import scipy.linalg

def solve_ge(A, b, pivot=True):
    """Solve Ax = b by Gaussian elimination, with or without partial pivoting."""
    A = np.array(A, dtype=float)
    b = np.array(b, dtype=float)
    n = len(b)
    for k in range(n - 1):
        if pivot:
            p = k + np.argmax(np.abs(A[k:, k]))   # row of the largest entry
            A[[k, p]] = A[[p, k]]                 # swap rows k and p
            b[[k, p]] = b[[p, k]]
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            m = A[i, k] / A[k, k]
            A[i, k:] -= m * A[k, k:]
            b[i] -= m * b[k]
    x = np.zeros(n)
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        x[i] = (b[i] - A[i, i + 1:] @ x[i + 1:]) / A[i, i]
    return x

A = [[1e-20, 1], [1, 1]]
b = [1, 2]
print(solve_ge(A, b, pivot=False))         # wrong: [0, 1]
print(solve_ge(A, b, pivot=True))          # right: [1, 1]

for k = 1:n-1

SciPy’s lu returns the permutation the other way round from MATLAB’s, so that \(\M{A} = \M{P}\M{L}\M{U}\); its transpose is the \(\M{P}\) of \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\).

25.4 Check your understanding

TipWith partial pivoting, can a multiplier ever be larger than 1 in size?

No. The pivot is the largest entry in its column (on and below the diagonal), so each multiplier, an entry divided by the pivot, is at most 1 in size.

TipDoes pivoting help with an ill-conditioned system?

Not with its conditioning. Pivoting stops the method from adding unnecessary error; it can’t remove the sensitivity built into the problem (Chapter 24).

TipWhy doesn’t np.linalg.solve let you turn pivoting off?

Because there is no reason to: pivoting costs almost nothing and protects against disaster. The only common exceptions, symmetric positive definite matrices, have their own solver, Cholesky.

25.5 Key ideas

  • Elimination without row swaps fails at a zero pivot, and can give wildly wrong answers at a small one, because large multipliers magnify rounding errors.
  • Partial pivoting swaps rows so that each pivot is the largest available in its column, which keeps every multiplier at most 1 in size.
  • The result is written \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\), and it is what library solvers compute.
  • Badly scaled rows can fool partial pivoting; scaled pivoting, or rescaling the equations first, fixes this.
  • Symmetric positive definite and diagonally dominant matrices need no pivoting at all.

25.7 In practice

  • Every general-purpose dense solver, in LAPACK and so in NumPy, SciPy, MATLAB, R and Julia, uses partial pivoting. You get it automatically.
  • Sparse solvers face a trade-off: swapping rows for accuracy can destroy the sparsity that makes them fast (Chapter 26). They often use threshold pivoting, accepting any pivot at least, say, 10% of the largest in its column.
  • Finite element stiffness matrices are symmetric positive definite, so their solvers use Cholesky and skip pivoting altogether.
  • Many matrices from physical problems are diagonally dominant: heat conduction, diffusion and circuit equations, for example. Solvers for their banded systems skip pivoting safely.

25.8 Going further

  • Higham’s Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms gives the full backward error analysis of elimination with pivoting, and Trefethen and Bau’s Numerical Linear Algebra discusses why large growth factors are so rare.
  • Rook pivoting, a compromise between partial and complete pivoting, searches along the pivot’s row and column alternately until it finds an entry largest in both.
  • For symmetric matrices that are not positive definite, the Bunch–Kaufman method pivots symmetrically, using \(1 \times 1\) and \(2 \times 2\) blocks, to keep the symmetry and halve the work.
