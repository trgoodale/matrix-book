31  Structures: pin-jointed trusses

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • set up the equilibrium equations of a truss as a linear system, and solve for the member forces
  • use rank to tell a sound structure from a mechanism
  • solve statically indeterminate trusses with the stiffness method

Builds on Chapter 10, Chapter 11, Chapter 16.

31.1 The problem

Look at almost any railway bridge, roof, crane or electricity pylon and you will see a truss: straight members joined at their ends to form triangles. Trusses are efficient because, when the loads act at the joints, each member is simply pulled (in tension) or pushed (in compression) along its length, with no bending. An engineer designing one must know the force in every member, to choose its size: a member in tension must not snap, and one in compression must not buckle.

Each joint must be in equilibrium: the forces on it from the members, the loads and the supports must add up to zero. That gives two linear equations per joint, one for each direction, and the member forces are the unknowns. A bridge with 30 joints gives 60 equations, which is a job for matrices.

31.2 The model

Equilibrium at each joint

Model the truss as a set of joints (nodes), at known positions, connected by members. Assume:

  • the members are joined by frictionless pins, so they can’t transmit bending;
  • loads are applied only at the joints;
  • each member carries a single axial force \(N\), the same along its length, counted positive in tension.

A member from joint \(i\) to joint \(j\), with unit vector \(\vect{e}\) pointing from \(i\) to \(j\), pulls joint \(i\) with force \(N\vect{e}\) (towards \(j\), if \(N > 0\)) and joint \(j\) with force \(-N\vect{e}\). Supports provide reactions: a pin can push in any direction, so it provides two unknown reaction components; a roller can only push perpendicular to the surface it rolls on, so it provides one (Figure 31.1).

At each joint, the sum of the member forces, the reactions and the applied load \(\vect{p}\) is zero. Collecting all the unknown member forces and reactions into one vector \(\vect{f}\), the \(2j\) equations for \(j\) joints form a linear system \[ \M{A}\vect{f} = -\vect{p}, \] where \(\vect{p}\) lists the applied loads, joint by joint. Column \(k\) of the equilibrium matrix \(\M{A}\) shows where unknown \(k\) pushes or pulls: a member’s column holds \(\vect{e}\) in the rows of joint \(i\) and \(-\vect{e}\) in the rows of joint \(j\), and a reaction’s column holds a single 1.

NoteExample 1: a triangular truss

Joints 1 at \((0, 0)\) and 2 at \((4, 0)\) (in metres) sit on a pin and a roller, and joint 3 at \((2, 2)\) carries a load of 10 kN downwards (Figure 31.2). Find the force in each member and the reactions.

Solution. Call the forces in members 1–2, 1–3 and 2–3 \(N_a\), \(N_b\) and \(N_c\). Their unit vectors are \((1, 0)\), \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)\) and \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-1, 1)\). The pin gives reactions \(R_{1x}\) and \(R_{1y}\), and the roller \(R_{2y}\). The six equations, two per joint, are \[ \mat{1 & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 & 0 & 0} \mat{N_a \\ N_b \\ N_c \\ R_{1x} \\ R_{1y} \\ R_{2y}} = \mat{0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 10}. \] The last two rows (joint 3) give \(N_b = N_c\) and \(N_b + N_c = -10\sqrt{2}\), so \(N_b = N_c = -5\sqrt{2} \approx -7.07\) kN: both sloping members are in compression. Row 3 then gives \(N_a = -N_c/\sqrt{2} = 5\) kN, in tension: the bottom member ties the feet together. Finally \(R_{1x} = 0\), \(R_{1y} = 5\) and \(R_{2y} = 5\) kN: each support carries half the load, as symmetry demands. Here \(m + r = 3 + 3 = 6 = 2j\), and the matrix is invertible, so these are the only forces that balance the load: the next section calls such a truss determinate.

Open this truss in the truss solver to check the member forces and reactions.

Determinate, indeterminate and mechanisms

The truss has \(m\) members and \(r\) reaction components, so \(\M{A}\) is \(2j \times (m + r)\). The fundamental subspaces of Chapter 16 say exactly what can happen.

  • If \(m + r = 2j\) and \(\M{A}\) is invertible, every load has exactly one set of member forces that balances it. The truss is statically determinate: equilibrium alone fixes the forces.
  • If \(\M{A}\) has rank less than \(2j\), its column space is not all of \(\R^{2j}\), so some loads can’t be balanced at all. The truss is a mechanism: it would fold up. This always happens if \(m + r < 2j\), but it can also happen with enough members, badly arranged.
  • If \(\M{A}\) has rank \(2j\) but \(m + r > 2j\), every load can be balanced, but in infinitely many ways: the null space of \(\M{A}\) contains sets of member forces that balance each other with no load at all (self-stress). The truss is statically indeterminate, with \(m + r - 2j\) redundant members. To find which forces actually occur, we need to know how the members stretch.

The simplest stable shape is the triangle. Adding a joint connected by two new members to a triangle keeps the count \(m + r = 2j\), which is why trusses are built from triangles.

NoteExample 2: a square is a mechanism

Four members join the corners of a square, on a pin and a roller. Show that it is a mechanism, and that one diagonal fixes it.

Solution. There are \(j = 4\) joints, \(m = 4\) members and \(r = 3\) reactions, so \(\M{A}\) is \(8 \times 7\). Its rank is at most 7, so its column space can’t be all of \(\R^8\): there are loads it can’t balance. Physically, the square can lean over into a parallelogram without any member changing length (Figure 31.3, left); a sideways push at the top has nothing to resist it. Adding a diagonal gives \(m + r = 8\), and the \(8 \times 8\) matrix is invertible: the square has been split into two triangles. Adding the second diagonal as well gives \(m + r = 9 > 8\): the truss is then indeterminate, with one redundant member. (Try Square and Doubly braced in the app below.)

The stiffness method

Real members stretch slightly under load. A member of length \(L\), cross-sectional area \(A\) and Young’s modulus \(E\) behaves like a stiff spring: its force is \(N = (EA/L)\,\delta\), where \(\delta\) is its extension. If the joints move by small displacements \(\vect{u}\), the extension of a member from \(i\) to \(j\) is \[ \delta = (\vect{u}_j - \vect{u}_i) \cdot \vect{e}. \] Writing \(\M{B}\) for the matrix that gives all the extensions, \(\vect{\delta} = \M{B}\vect{u}\), and \(\M{D}\) for the diagonal matrix of member stiffnesses \(EA/L\), the member forces are \(\vect{N} = \M{D}\M{B}\vect{u}\). Equilibrium at the free joints turns out to use the transpose of the same matrix, \(\M{B}\T\vect{N} = \vect{p}\) (exercise 5), so \[ \M{K}\vect{u} = \vect{p}, \qquad \M{K} = \M{B}\T\M{D}\M{B}. \]

The stiffness matrix \(\M{K}\) is symmetric, and positive definite unless the truss is a mechanism (Chapter 20). Solve for the displacements \(\vect{u}\), then find the forces from \(\vect{N} = \M{D}\M{B}\vect{u}\). This works for determinate and indeterminate trusses alike, and it gives the deflections too. It is the method that structural analysis software uses, and the starting point of the finite element method.

Each member contributes a \(4 \times 4\) block, \[ \frac{EA}{L}\mat{\vect{e}\vect{e}\T & -\vect{e}\vect{e}\T \\ -\vect{e}\vect{e}\T & \vect{e}\vect{e}\T}, \] to the rows and columns of its two joints, so \(\M{K}\) can be assembled member by member.

NoteExample 3: an indeterminate truss by the stiffness method

A joint at the origin hangs from three supports, at \((-1, 1)\), \((0, 1)\) and \((1, 1)\), by three bars with the same \(EA\), and carries a 10 kN load downwards (Figure 31.4). Find the bar forces.

Solution. There are only 2 equilibrium equations (at the free joint) and 3 unknown forces, so equilibrium alone can’t decide how the load is shared. Taking each unit vector from the free joint towards its support, \(\vect{e}_A = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-1, 1)\), \(\vect{e}_B = (0, 1)\) and \(\vect{e}_C = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)\), with lengths \(\sqrt{2}\), 1 and \(\sqrt{2}\). Only the free joint moves, so \[ \M{K} = \sum\frac{EA}{L}\vect{e}\vect{e}\T = EA\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{2}\mat{1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1} + \mat{0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{2}\mat{1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1}\right) = EA\mat{0.707 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.707}. \] Solving \(\M{K}\vect{u} = (0, -10)\T\) gives \(\vect{u} = \bigl(0, \; -10/(1.707\,EA)\bigr)\): the joint moves straight down. A bar’s extension is \(-\vect{u} \cdot \vect{e}\) (its far end is fixed), so the forces are \[ N_B = \frac{EA}{1} \cdot \frac{10}{1.707\,EA} = 5.86 \text{ kN}, \qquad N_A = N_C = \frac{EA}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{10}{1.707\,EA} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = 2.93 \text{ kN}, \] all in tension. Check: the vertical components add up to \(5.86 + 2 \times 2.93/\sqrt{2} = 10\). ✓ The short vertical bar is the stiffest, so it takes the largest share; making it stiffer still would give it even more (exercise 6).

31.4 In code

The function below builds the equilibrium matrix from lists of joints, members and supports, following the model above, and solves it. For the Pratt bridge it prints the force in each member with the 20 kN load at the middle of the span.

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

def truss(P, members, supports, loads):
    """Equilibrium matrix A and right-hand side for a pin-jointed truss.
    Unknowns: member forces (tension positive), then reactions."""
    P = np.array(P, dtype=float)
    reactions = [(j, d) for j, kind in supports.items()
                 for d in ([0, 1] if kind == "pin" else [1])]
    A = np.zeros((2 * len(P), len(members) + len(reactions)))
    for k, (i, j) in enumerate(members):
        e = (P[j] - P[i]) / np.linalg.norm(P[j] - P[i])
        A[2 * i:2 * i + 2, k] += e        # member pulls joint i towards j
        A[2 * j:2 * j + 2, k] -= e        # and joint j towards i
    for r, (j, d) in enumerate(reactions):
        A[2 * j + d, len(members) + r] = 1
    b = np.zeros(2 * len(P))
    for j, (fx, fy) in loads.items():
        b[2 * j:2 * j + 2] = [-fx, -fy]
    return A, b

# Example 1
A, b = truss([[0, 0], [4, 0], [2, 2]], [(0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 2)],
             {0: "pin", 1: "roller"}, {2: (0, -10)})
print(np.linalg.solve(A, b))

For the Pratt bridge, the bottom chord is in tension (10 kN), the top chord in compression (20 kN), the end posts in compression and the interior diagonals in tension (14.14 kN), and the three verticals carry nothing for this load. Engineers favour the Pratt layout for steel bridges because its long diagonals are in tension, where slender members are strong, while the short verticals take any compression.

31.5 Check your understanding

TipA plane truss has 5 joints and 7 members, and is held by a pin and a roller. Is it statically determinate?

The count is right: \(m + r = 7 + 3 = 10 = 2j\). So it is determinate if its equilibrium matrix is invertible. The count alone can’t tell you that: badly placed members can leave it a mechanism, with a square \(\M{A}\) of rank less than 10.

TipWhy does a square frame of four members fold up, and what is the cheapest fix?

With 4 joints, 4 members and 3 reactions, \(m + r = 7 < 8 = 2j\), so \(\M{A}\) has rank less than 8 and some loads can’t be balanced: the square is a mechanism. One diagonal member makes two triangles, and \(m + r = 8\).

TipWhy is the stiffness matrix \(\M{K} = \M{B}\T\M{D}\M{B}\) positive definite for a truss that is not a mechanism?

\(\vect{u}\T\M{K}\vect{u} = (\M{B}\vect{u})\T\M{D}(\M{B}\vect{u}) = \sum (EA/L)\,\delta^2 \ge 0\), the stored strain energy. It is zero only if no member stretches, \(\M{B}\vect{u} = \zerovec\), and in a truss that is not a mechanism the only such displacement is \(\vect{u} = \zerovec\).

31.6 Key ideas

  • Equilibrium at every joint gives two linear equations, with the member forces and support reactions as the unknowns.
  • A truss is statically determinate when its equilibrium matrix is square and invertible; a mechanism is a structure whose matrix is rank deficient.
  • Counting \(m + r\) against \(2j\) is a quick check, but only the rank settles the question.
  • Indeterminate trusses need the stiffness method, \(\M{K}\vect{u} = \vect{f}\) with \(\M{K} = \M{B}\T\M{D}\M{B}\), which is symmetric positive definite for a structure that is not a mechanism.

31.8 Going further

  • Strang’s Introduction to Linear Algebra and Computational Science and Engineering build a whole theory of applied mathematics on the pattern \(\M{B}\T\M{D}\M{B}\), which appears again in electrical circuits (Chapter 32), springs and heat flow.
  • The finite element method generalises the stiffness method to beams, plates, shells and solids, by dividing a structure into small elements, each with its own stiffness matrix, and assembling them in exactly the same way. Commercial packages such as Abaqus and ANSYS solve systems with millions of unknowns this way, with the sparse solvers of Chapter 26.
  • Trusses in three dimensions (space frames, such as pylons and roof grids) work in the same way, with three equations per joint.
  • Members in compression can buckle, failing sideways at a load far below their crushing strength. Finding buckling loads leads to an eigenvalue problem for the stiffness matrix (Chapter 29).
