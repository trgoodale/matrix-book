18  Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • find the eigenvalues of a matrix from its characteristic equation, and its eigenvectors from null spaces
  • use the trace and determinant to check eigenvalues
  • recognise repeated eigenvalues, including matrices without enough eigenvectors
  • interpret complex eigenvalues as rotation combined with stretching

Builds on Chapter 6, Chapter 9, Chapter 16.

Chapter 9 found the lines that a \(2 \times 2\) matrix maps onto themselves, with a formula that only works in two dimensions. Along such a line the matrix just stretches: \(\M{A}\vect{v} = \lambda\vect{v}\). The vectors \(\vect{v}\) are eigenvectors and the stretch factors \(\lambda\) are eigenvalues. This chapter gives the method that works for a square matrix of any size.

Eigenvalues are among the most useful numbers in applied mathematics. They give the natural frequencies of a vibrating structure, the growth rate of a population, the stability of a control system, the principal stresses in a loaded beam and the most important directions in a data set. The rest of Part IV is built on them.

18.1 Build intuition

The invariant line finder from Chapter 9 shows the same idea. Turn the vector \(\vect{v}\) and watch for the directions where \(\M{A}\vect{v}\) lines up with \(\vect{v}\). Each such direction is an eigenvector, and the stretch factor shown is its eigenvalue.

Open the invariant line finder in its own tab

Things to try:

  • For each preset, predict how many eigenvector directions there will be before you sweep.
  • Rotate 90° has none. What do you think its eigenvalues are? (This chapter’s answer involves complex numbers.)
  • Shear has only one eigenvector direction, although a \(2 \times 2\) matrix usually has two. Keep this example in mind for the section on repeated eigenvalues.

18.2 The ideas

Directions that are only stretched

A non-zero vector \(\vect{v}\) is an eigenvector of the square matrix \(\M{A}\), with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), if \[ \M{A}\vect{v} = \lambda\vect{v}. \] The matrix sends \(\vect{v}\) to a multiple of itself, so it stays on the same line through the origin. The meaning of \(\lambda\) is as in Chapter 9: points along that line are stretched (\(\lambda > 1\)), shrunk (\(0 < \lambda < 1\)), left alone (\(\lambda = 1\)), squashed to the origin (\(\lambda = 0\)) or flipped (\(\lambda < 0\)).

The zero vector is not allowed as an eigenvector (it would satisfy the equation for every \(\lambda\)), but \(\lambda = 0\) is a perfectly good eigenvalue. Any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector, with the same eigenvalue, so it is really a direction that is being found. Together with \(\zerovec\), all the eigenvectors for one eigenvalue form a subspace (Chapter 14), called its eigenspace.

The characteristic equation

Rewrite \(\M{A}\vect{v} = \lambda\vect{v}\) as \(\M{A}\vect{v} - \lambda\M{I}\vect{v} = \zerovec\), that is, \[ (\M{A} - \lambda\M{I})\vect{v} = \zerovec. \] This homogeneous system has a non-zero solution exactly when \(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}\) is singular (Chapter 10), so the eigenvalues are the solutions of the characteristic equation \[ \det(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}) = 0. \tag{18.1}\] For an \(n \times n\) matrix, \(\det(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I})\) is a polynomial in \(\lambda\) of degree \(n\), the characteristic polynomial. So an \(n \times n\) matrix has exactly \(n\) eigenvalues, provided you count repeated ones as often as they repeat and allow complex numbers.

For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(\M{A} = \mat{a & b \\ c & d}\), \[ \det\mat{a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda} = \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc). \] The coefficient \(a + d\) is the trace of \(\M{A}\), written \(\operatorname{tr}\M{A}\): the sum of its diagonal entries. So \[ \lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}\M{A})\,\lambda + \det\M{A} = 0. \tag{18.2}\] This gives a useful check in any size: the eigenvalues add up to the trace, and multiply to give the determinant (a proof is in Section C.11).

WarningWatch out: row reduction changes eigenvalues

Find the eigenvalues from \(\M{A}\) itself. Row operations change them: \(\mat{2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3}\) has eigenvalues 2 and 3, but halving its first row gives \(\mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3}\), with eigenvalues 1 and 3. Row reduce only \(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}\), once you know \(\lambda\), to find the eigenvectors.

Finding the eigenvectors

For each eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the eigenvectors are the non-zero solutions of \((\M{A} - \lambda\M{I})\vect{v} = \zerovec\): the null space of \(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}\) (Chapter 16). Row reduce, and read the solutions off in terms of free variables, as in Chapter 12. Because \(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}\) is singular, there is always at least one free variable. If there isn’t, the eigenvalue is wrong.

NoteExample 1: a \(2 \times 2\) matrix

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(\M{A} = \mat{4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3}\).

Solution. The trace is 7 and the determinant is \(12 - 2 = 10\), so by Equation 18.2, \[ \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2) = 0, \] giving \(\lambda_1 = 5\) and \(\lambda_2 = 2\). Check: \(5 + 2 = 7\), the trace, and \(5 \times 2 = 10\), the determinant. ✓

For \(\lambda_1 = 5\): \(\M{A} - 5\M{I} = \mat{-1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2}\). Both rows say \(x = y\), so \(\vect{v}_1 = (1, 1)\T\).

For \(\lambda_2 = 2\): \(\M{A} - 2\M{I} = \mat{2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1}\). Both rows say \(2x + y = 0\), so \(\vect{v}_2 = (1, -2)\T\).

Check: \(\M{A}(1, -2)\T = (4 - 2, 2 - 6)\T = (2, -4)\T = 2(1, -2)\T\). ✓

Quick facts

Some eigenvalues can be seen without any calculation.

  • A triangular matrix (including a diagonal one) has its diagonal entries as its eigenvalues, because \(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}\) is triangular and its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries.
  • \(\lambda = 0\) is an eigenvalue exactly when \(\M{A}\) is singular, since then \(\M{A}\vect{v} = 0\vect{v}\) has a non-zero solution.
  • If \(\M{A}\vect{v} = \lambda\vect{v}\), then \(\M{A}^2\vect{v} = \M{A}(\lambda\vect{v}) = \lambda^2\vect{v}\), and in general \(\M{A}^k\) has eigenvalue \(\lambda^k\) with the same eigenvector. If \(\M{A}\) is invertible, \(\M{A}^{-1}\) has eigenvalue \(1/\lambda\).
  • \(\M{A}\) and \(\M{A}\T\) have the same eigenvalues (but usually different eigenvectors), since a matrix and its transpose have the same determinant.

Repeated eigenvalues

When an eigenvalue is a repeated root of the characteristic polynomial, its eigenspace may or may not have as many dimensions as the repeat count. The identity matrix \(\M{I}\) has \(\lambda = 1\) twice, and every vector is an eigenvector: a whole plane of them. But the shear \(\mat{1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1}\) also has \(\lambda = 1\) twice, and only the \(x\)-axis is an eigenvector direction (Chapter 9, Example 4).

The number of times \(\lambda\) repeats as a root is its algebraic multiplicity; the dimension of its eigenspace is its geometric multiplicity. The geometric multiplicity is always at least 1 and never more than the algebraic multiplicity. A matrix where it is smaller, like the shear, is called defective: it doesn’t have enough independent eigenvectors to make a basis, which matters in Chapter 19.

NoteExample 2: a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with a repeated eigenvalue

Find the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of \(\M{A} = \mat{2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2}\).

Solution. Expanding \(\det(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I})\) directly is possible, but a shortcut is quicker. \(\M{A} - \M{I}\) has every entry equal to 1, so it has rank 1 and is singular: \(\lambda = 1\) is an eigenvalue, and its eigenspace has dimension \(3 - 1 = 2\). So \(\lambda = 1\) appears at least twice. The trace is 6, so the third eigenvalue is \(6 - 1 - 1 = 4\). (Check with the determinant: \(1 \times 1 \times 4 = 4 = \det\M{A}\).)

For \(\lambda = 1\): \((\M{A} - \M{I})\vect{v} = \zerovec\) is the single equation \(x + y + z = 0\). With \(y\) and \(z\) free, a basis is \((-1, 1, 0)\T\) and \((-1, 0, 1)\T\): the eigenspace is a plane.

For \(\lambda = 4\): \(\M{A} - 4\M{I} = \mat{-2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & -2}\), whose rows each add up to zero, so \(\vect{v} = (1, 1, 1)\T\) works. The eigenspace is the line through it.

The repeated eigenvalue has a two-dimensional eigenspace, so this matrix is not defective. (Notice also that \((1, 1, 1)\) is at right angles to the whole plane \(x + y + z = 0\). That is no accident: \(\M{A}\) is symmetric, Chapter 20.)

NoteExample 3: a defective matrix

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the shear \(\M{H} = \mat{1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1}\).

Solution. It is triangular, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries: \(\lambda = 1\), twice. \(\M{H} - \M{I} = \mat{0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0}\) says \(y = 0\), so the eigenvectors are the multiples of \((1, 0)\T\) only. The algebraic multiplicity is 2 but the geometric multiplicity is 1: \(\M{H}\) is defective.

Complex eigenvalues

The rotation \(\M{R}(\theta) = \mat{\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta}\) turns every direction, so for \(\theta\) other than \(0°\) or \(180°\) it has no real eigenvectors. Its characteristic equation is \[ \lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta\,\lambda + 1 = 0, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \lambda = \cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta = e^{\pm i\theta}, \] a pair of complex numbers (see the Refresher for complex numbers). Their angle is the angle of rotation, and their size, \(\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1\), says that lengths don’t change.

In general, a real matrix with complex eigenvalues has them in conjugate pairs, \(\alpha \pm i\beta\), because the characteristic polynomial has real coefficients. For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, such a pair means the matrix acts like a rotation through the angle of \(\lambda\) combined with a stretch by \(\lvert\lambda\rvert = \sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}\), as seen in suitable coordinates. The eigenvectors are complex too. You won’t need them in this book except in passing, but the eigenvalues themselves are vital: in Chapter 23 they become oscillation frequencies.

NoteExample 4: complex eigenvalues

Find the eigenvalues of \(\M{A} = \mat{1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1}\) and interpret them.

Solution. The trace is 2 and the determinant is 2, so \(\lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 2 = 0\), and \[ \lambda = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 8}}{2} = 1 \pm i. \] Each has size \(\lvert\lambda\rvert = \sqrt{2}\) and angle \(45°\). Indeed \(\M{A} = \sqrt{2}\,\M{R}(45°)\): it rotates by \(45°\) and stretches by \(\sqrt{2}\). No real direction is left on its own line, so there are no real eigenvectors.

18.3 In code

Run this chapter’s code yourself: open the notebook in Colab, or download the Jupyter notebook or the MATLAB script.

np.linalg.eig and MATLAB’s eig return the eigenvalues, and the eigenvectors as the columns of a matrix in the same order, each scaled to length 1. They don’t use the characteristic polynomial, which would be slow and inaccurate for large matrices. Instead they use the QR algorithm of Chapter 29. The order of the eigenvalues, and the sign of each eigenvector, may differ from a hand calculation.

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[4, 1], [2, 3]])
values, vectors = np.linalg.eig(A)
values, vectors = values.real, vectors.real   # recent NumPy returns complex numbers
print(values)
print(vectors)                   # columns: (1, 1) and (1, -2), scaled to length 1

print(np.allclose(A @ vectors, vectors * values))   # A v = lambda v for each column

print(np.linalg.eigvals(np.array([[1, -1], [1, 1]])))   # complex: 1 + i and 1 - i

18.4 Check your understanding

TipA \(3 \times 3\) matrix has eigenvalues 2, 2 and \(-1\). What are its trace and determinant?

Trace \(2 + 2 - 1 = 3\); determinant \(2 \times 2 \times (-1) = -4\).

TipIf 0 is an eigenvalue of \(\M{A}\), what can you say about \(\M{A}\)?

It is singular: it has no inverse, and \(\det\M{A} = 0\).

TipCan a real \(3 \times 3\) matrix have no real eigenvalues?

No. Complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs, so with three eigenvalues at least one must be real. (Geometrically: every rotation of space has an axis.)

18.5 Key ideas

  • An eigenvector of \(\M{A}\) is a non-zero vector whose direction \(\M{A}\) doesn’t change: \(\M{A}\vect{v} = \lambda\vect{v}\), where \(\lambda\) is its eigenvalue.
  • The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic equation \(\det(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}) = 0\). The eigenvectors for each \(\lambda\) form the null space of \(\M{A} - \lambda\M{I}\).
  • The eigenvalues add up to the trace and multiply to the determinant, which makes a quick check.
  • A repeated eigenvalue may have fewer independent eigenvectors than its multiplicity; such a matrix is called defective.
  • Complex eigenvalues of a real matrix come in conjugate pairs, and describe a rotation combined with a stretch.

18.7 In practice

  • Vibration: every structure, from a guitar string to a bridge or an aircraft wing, has natural frequencies at which it vibrates freely, found from the eigenvalues of its stiffness and mass matrices. Engineers keep them away from the frequencies of wind, traffic, engines or footsteps, to avoid resonance. The Millennium Bridge in London swayed on its opening day in 2000 because a sideways natural frequency matched the rhythm of people walking.
  • Stability: whether a control system, an electrical network or an ecosystem returns to equilibrium after a disturbance depends on the eigenvalues of its linearised equations (Chapter 23).
  • Principal stresses: the eigenvalues of the stress matrix at a point of a loaded material are the largest and smallest normal stresses there, and the eigenvectors are their directions (Chapter 20).
  • Rankings: Google’s original PageRank was the eigenvector, with eigenvalue 1, of a huge matrix describing the links between web pages (Chapter 35).
  • Quantum mechanics: the possible energies of an atom or molecule are the eigenvalues of a matrix (or operator) called the Hamiltonian (Chapter 38).

18.8 Going further

  • Never compute eigenvalues of large matrices from the characteristic polynomial: the roots of a polynomial can be extremely sensitive to tiny changes in its coefficients. Chapter 29 describes the methods that software uses instead.
  • Gershgorin’s theorem gives a quick estimate: every eigenvalue lies within a distance \(\sum_{j \neq i}\lvert a_{ij}\rvert\) of some diagonal entry \(a_{ii}\).
  • Defective matrices can’t be diagonalised, but they have a Jordan form, which is almost diagonal (Chapter 19).
  • Proofs that the eigenvalues add to the trace and multiply to the determinant: Section C.11.
