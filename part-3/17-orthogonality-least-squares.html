17  Orthogonality and least squares

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • use dot products to measure lengths, angles and right angles in \(\R^n\)
  • project a vector onto a line, and onto the column space of a matrix
  • find the least-squares solution of an inconsistent system with the normal equations, and use it to fit models to data
  • build an orthonormal basis with the Gram–Schmidt process

Builds on Chapter 7, Chapter 16, and the dot product from the Refresher.

Measured data never fits a model exactly. Five measurements of a spring under five loads give five equations for two unknowns (its natural length and its stretchiness), and because every measurement has a small error, no straight line passes through all five points. The system has no solution. But giving up would be absurd: there is clearly a best line. This chapter makes “best” precise, as the line that makes the total squared error as small as possible, and finds it with one linear system, the normal equations. The key idea is a right angle, so the chapter starts with dot products.

17.1 Build intuition

Adjust the blue line to fit the points as well as you can by eye. Each vertical dashed line is the error at one point, and the shaded squares show those errors squared. The least-squares line is the one with the smallest total area of squares.

17.2 The ideas

Lengths, angles and right angles in \(\R^n\)

The dot product of the Refresher works for vectors of any length: \[ \vect{x} \cdot \vect{y} = x_1y_1 + x_2y_2 + \dots + x_ny_n = \vect{x}\T\vect{y}. \] The second form, a row times a column, is how it usually appears in matrix calculations. From it come:

  • the length (or norm) \(\lVert\vect{x}\rVert = \sqrt{\vect{x} \cdot \vect{x}}\), Pythagoras in \(n\) dimensions;
  • the distance between \(\vect{x}\) and \(\vect{y}\), which is \(\lVert\vect{x} - \vect{y}\rVert\);
  • the angle \(\theta\) between \(\vect{x}\) and \(\vect{y}\), from \(\vect{x} \cdot \vect{y} = \lVert\vect{x}\rVert\,\lVert\vect{y}\rVert\cos\theta\).

That last formula needs \(\lvert\vect{x} \cdot \vect{y}\rvert \le \lVert\vect{x}\rVert\,\lVert\vect{y}\rVert\), so that \(\cos\theta\) lies between \(-1\) and 1. This is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and it holds in any number of dimensions (a proof is in Section C.10).

Two vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular) when \(\vect{x} \cdot \vect{y} = 0\). For orthogonal vectors, Pythagoras’ theorem holds: \[ \lVert\vect{x} + \vect{y}\rVert^2 = (\vect{x} + \vect{y}) \cdot (\vect{x} + \vect{y}) = \lVert\vect{x}\rVert^2 + 2\,\vect{x} \cdot \vect{y} + \lVert\vect{y}\rVert^2 = \lVert\vect{x}\rVert^2 + \lVert\vect{y}\rVert^2. \]

Projection onto a line

The closest point to \(\vect{b}\) on the line through the origin in the direction \(\vect{a}\) is found by dropping a perpendicular. Call it \(\vect{p} = \hat{x}\,\vect{a}\). The error \(\vect{e} = \vect{b} - \vect{p}\) must be at right angles to \(\vect{a}\): \[ \vect{a} \cdot (\vect{b} - \hat{x}\,\vect{a}) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{x} = \frac{\vect{a} \cdot \vect{b}}{\vect{a} \cdot \vect{a}}, \qquad \vect{p} = \frac{\vect{a} \cdot \vect{b}}{\vect{a} \cdot \vect{a}}\,\vect{a}. \tag{17.1}\] This \(\vect{p}\) is the projection of \(\vect{b}\) onto the line. It really is the closest point: any other point \(\vect{q}\) on the line gives \(\vect{b} - \vect{q} = \vect{e} + (\vect{p} - \vect{q})\), a sum of two orthogonal vectors, so by Pythagoras \(\lVert\vect{b} - \vect{q}\rVert^2 = \lVert\vect{e}\rVert^2 + \lVert\vect{p} - \vect{q}\rVert^2\), which is bigger. You met this as the “shadow” of one vector on another in the dot product view of the vector explorer.

NoteExample 1: projection onto a line

Project \(\vect{b} = (3, 1)\T\) onto the line through \(\vect{a} = (1, 2)\T\), and find the distance from \(\vect{b}\) to the line.

Solution. \(\vect{a} \cdot \vect{b} = 3 + 2 = 5\) and \(\vect{a} \cdot \vect{a} = 1 + 4 = 5\), so by Equation 17.1, \(\vect{p} = \tfrac{5}{5}\vect{a} = (1, 2)\T\). The error is \(\vect{e} = \vect{b} - \vect{p} = (2, -1)\T\). Check that it is at right angles to the line: \(\vect{e} \cdot \vect{a} = 2 - 2 = 0\). ✓ The distance from \(\vect{b}\) to the line is \(\lVert\vect{e}\rVert = \sqrt{5}\).

Projection onto a subspace

Now project \(\vect{b}\) onto a bigger subspace: the column space of a matrix \(\M{A}\) with independent columns (Chapter 16). The closest point is \(\vect{p} = \M{A}\hat{\vect{x}}\) for some \(\hat{\vect{x}}\), and the same argument says the error \(\vect{e} = \vect{b} - \M{A}\hat{\vect{x}}\) must be at right angles to the whole subspace, so to every column of \(\M{A}\). Dotting with every column at once is multiplying by \(\M{A}\T\): \[ \M{A}\T(\vect{b} - \M{A}\hat{\vect{x}}) = \zerovec \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \M{A}\T\M{A}\,\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{A}\T\vect{b}. \tag{17.2}\] These are the normal equations (“normal” is another word for perpendicular). \(\M{A}\T\M{A}\) is a square matrix, with one row and column for each column of \(\M{A}\), and it is invertible exactly when the columns of \(\M{A}\) are independent (Chapter 16, Exercise 6). Then the projection is \[ \vect{p} = \M{A}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{A}(\M{A}\T\M{A})^{-1}\M{A}\T\vect{b}. \] The matrix \(\M{P} = \M{A}(\M{A}\T\M{A})^{-1}\M{A}\T\) is the projection matrix onto the column space. Projecting twice is the same as projecting once, so \(\M{P}^2 = \M{P}\).

Least squares

Suppose \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) has no solution, because \(\vect{b}\) is not in the column space. The least-squares solution is the \(\hat{\vect{x}}\) that makes \(\M{A}\hat{\vect{x}}\) as close as possible to \(\vect{b}\): it minimises \[ \lVert\M{A}\vect{x} - \vect{b}\rVert^2 = \text{the sum of the squares of the errors in each equation}. \] The closest point of the column space to \(\vect{b}\) is its projection, so the least-squares solution is the solution of the normal equations Equation 17.2. When \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) does have a solution, the normal equations give it, with zero error.

The most common use is fitting a model to data. To fit a straight line \(y = c + mt\) to points \((t_1, y_1), \dots, (t_k, y_k)\), ask for every point to lie on the line: \[ \mat{1 & t_1 \\ 1 & t_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_k}\mat{c \\ m} = \mat{y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_k}. \] With more points than unknowns, this generally has no solution, and least squares gives the line of best fit. The method works for any model in which the unknown coefficients appear linearly, even if the model is curved. A parabola \(y = c_0 + c_1t + c_2t^2\) just needs a third column, holding \(t_i^2\).

Why squares, rather than the plain size of the errors? Squaring makes every error count as positive, gives a smooth quantity that calculus can minimise (Chapter 22 finds the normal equations that way too), and matches the geometry of distance. It has one weakness, which the app shows: a single wild point has a large squared error, so it can pull the line a long way.

NoteExample 2: calibrating a spring

A spring is hung with loads of 0, 1, 2 and 3 newtons, and its length is measured as 1, 2, 4 and 5 cm. Fit the straight line \(y = c + mt\), where \(t\) is the load and \(y\) the length.

Solution. Asking every point to lie on the line gives four equations for two unknowns: \[ \M{A} = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3}, \qquad \vect{y} = \mat{1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \\ 5}. \] The normal equations need \[ \M{A}\T\M{A} = \mat{4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14}, \qquad \M{A}\T\vect{y} = \mat{12 \\ 25}. \] (The entries are the number of points, the sum of the \(t_i\), the sum of the \(t_i^2\), the sum of the \(y_i\) and the sum of the \(t_iy_i\).) Solving \(4c + 6m = 12\) and \(6c + 14m = 25\) gives \(c = 0.9\) and \(m = 1.4\), so the line of best fit is \[ y = 0.9 + 1.4t. \] The spring is about 0.9 cm long unloaded, and stretches about 1.4 cm per newton. The errors are \(\vect{e} = \vect{y} - \M{A}\hat{\vect{x}} = (0.1, -0.3, 0.3, -0.1)\T\), so the sum of squared errors is \(0.01 + 0.09 + 0.09 + 0.01 = 0.2\). As the theory says, \(\vect{e}\) is at right angles to both columns: its entries add up to 0, and \(0(0.1) + 1(-0.3) + 2(0.3) + 3(-0.1) = 0\). This is the Spring example in the app.

Orthonormal bases and Gram–Schmidt

Vectors \(\vect{q}_1, \dots, \vect{q}_k\) are orthonormal if each has length 1 and each pair is orthogonal. Put them in the columns of a matrix \(\M{Q}\). The entries of \(\M{Q}\T\M{Q}\) are the dot products \(\vect{q}_i \cdot \vect{q}_j\), so orthonormality says exactly \[ \M{Q}\T\M{Q} = \M{I}. \] (A square \(\M{Q}\) like this is an orthogonal matrix, as in Chapter 7.) Orthonormal bases make everything easy:

  • the coordinates of \(\vect{v}\) in an orthonormal basis are just dot products, \(c_i = \vect{q}_i \cdot \vect{v}\), with no system to solve;
  • the normal equations become \(\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{Q}\T\vect{b}\), and the projection onto the column space is \(\vect{p} = \M{Q}\M{Q}\T\vect{b} = (\vect{q}_1 \cdot \vect{b})\vect{q}_1 + \dots + (\vect{q}_k \cdot \vect{b})\vect{q}_k\), a sum of projections onto lines.

The Gram–Schmidt process turns any independent vectors \(\vect{a}_1, \dots, \vect{a}_k\) into orthonormal ones with the same span. Take each vector in turn, subtract its projections onto the orthonormal vectors found so far, and scale what is left to length 1: \[ \vect{v}_j = \vect{a}_j - (\vect{q}_1 \cdot \vect{a}_j)\,\vect{q}_1 - \dots - (\vect{q}_{j-1} \cdot \vect{a}_j)\,\vect{q}_{j-1}, \qquad \vect{q}_j = \frac{\vect{v}_j}{\lVert\vect{v}_j\rVert}. \] What is left, \(\vect{v}_j\), is the part of \(\vect{a}_j\) at right angles to all the earlier vectors. Recording the numbers used along the way writes \(\M{A} = \M{Q}\M{R}\), with \(\M{R}\) upper triangular, the QR factorisation. It is the reliable way to solve least-squares problems on a computer (Chapter 30).

NoteExample 3: Gram–Schmidt, and a projection onto a plane

Find an orthonormal basis for the plane spanned by \(\vect{a}_1 = (1, 1, 0)\T\) and \(\vect{a}_2 = (1, 0, 1)\T\), and use it to project \(\vect{b} = (1, 2, 3)\T\) onto the plane.

Solution. First, \(\lVert\vect{a}_1\rVert = \sqrt{2}\), so \(\vect{q}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1, 0)\T\). Next, remove from \(\vect{a}_2\) its projection onto \(\vect{q}_1\). Since \(\vect{q}_1 \cdot \vect{a}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\), \[ \vect{v}_2 = \vect{a}_2 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\vect{q}_1 = (1, 0, 1)\T - \tfrac{1}{2}(1, 1, 0)\T = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}, 1\right)\T. \] Its length is \(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} + 1} = \sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}}\), so \(\vect{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1, -1, 2)\T\). Check: \(\vect{q}_1 \cdot \vect{q}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{12}}(1 - 1 + 0) = 0\). ✓

With an orthonormal basis, the projection is a sum of projections onto lines. \(\vect{q}_1 \cdot \vect{b} = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\) and \(\vect{q}_2 \cdot \vect{b} = \tfrac{5}{\sqrt{6}}\), so \[ \vect{p} = \tfrac{3}{2}(1, 1, 0)\T + \tfrac{5}{6}(1, -1, 2)\T = \left(\tfrac{7}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{5}{3}\right)\T. \] The error \(\vect{b} - \vect{p} = \tfrac{4}{3}(-1, 1, 1)\T\) is at right angles to both \(\vect{a}_1\) and \(\vect{a}_2\), as it should be.

17.3 In code

For least squares, use a solver that works on \(\M{A}\) directly rather than forming \(\M{A}\T\M{A}\): NumPy’s lstsq, or MATLAB’s backslash, which automatically gives the least-squares solution when \(\M{A}\) has more rows than columns. They use the QR factorisation, which is more accurate (Chapter 30). For fitting polynomials there is also polyfit, which lists the coefficients from the highest power down.

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

t = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3])
y = np.array([1, 2, 4, 5])
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t])        # columns: 1 and t

x, sse, rank, sv = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)
print(x, sse)                                    # c and m, and the sum of squared errors

print(np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ y))         # the normal equations: same answer
print(np.polyfit(t, y, 1))                       # slope first, then intercept

Q, R = np.linalg.qr(np.array([[1, 1], [1, 0], [0, 1]]))
print(np.round(Q, 4) + 0)                        # orthonormal columns (Example 3, up to sign)

17.4 Check your understanding

TipWhat is the projection of \(\vect{b}\) onto a line if \(\vect{b}\) is already on the line? If \(\vect{b}\) is at right angles to the line?

\(\vect{b}\) itself; and \(\zerovec\).

TipWhy does the least-squares error vector have to be at right angles to every column of \(\M{A}\)?

If it weren’t, moving \(\M{A}\hat{\vect{x}}\) a little along that column would bring it closer to \(\vect{b}\), so it wouldn’t be the closest point.

TipIf the columns of \(\M{Q}\) are orthonormal, what is \(\M{Q}\T\M{Q}\)?

The identity matrix.

17.5 Key ideas

  • The dot product measures length, \(\lVert\vect{v}\rVert = \sqrt{\vect{v}\cdot\vect{v}}\), and angle, \(\cos\theta = \vect{u}\cdot\vect{v}/(\lVert\vect{u}\rVert\lVert\vect{v}\rVert)\), in any number of dimensions. Vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero.
  • Projecting \(\vect{b}\) onto a line or subspace gives its closest point there, and the error is at right angles to the subspace.
  • When \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) has no solution, the least-squares solution solves the normal equations \(\M{A}\T\M{A}\hat{\vect{x}} = \M{A}\T\vect{b}\), and makes \(\M{A}\hat{\vect{x}}\) the projection of \(\vect{b}\) onto the column space.
  • Fitting a line or curve to data is a least-squares problem.
  • Gram–Schmidt turns any basis into an orthonormal one, in which coordinates and projections are just dot products.

17.7 In practice

  • Calibration: instruments from thermometers to strain gauges are calibrated by fitting a line or curve to readings at known values, exactly as in Example 2.
  • Statistics: linear regression is least squares. Each row of \(\M{A}\) is one observation, each column one explanatory variable, and \(\hat{\vect{x}}\) holds the estimated effects (Chapter 37).
  • GPS: a receiver measures its distance to more satellites than it strictly needs, with small errors in each. It finds its position by least squares (after a step that makes the equations linear, Chapter 28), and the extra satellites improve the accuracy.
  • Surveying and image processing: networks of measured angles and distances are adjusted by least squares so that they fit together consistently, and least squares underlies many methods for removing blur and noise from images.

17.8 Going further

  • Weighted least squares gives more reliable measurements more influence, by minimising \(\sum w_i e_i^2\) with weights \(w_i\). Robust methods reduce the influence of outliers, for example by minimising \(\sum \lvert e_i\rvert\) instead.
  • Forming \(\M{A}\T\M{A}\) squares the condition number of the problem (Chapter 24), so it can lose accuracy when the columns of \(\M{A}\) are nearly dependent. The QR factorisation avoids this (Chapter 30).
  • When the columns of \(\M{A}\) are dependent, there are many least-squares solutions. The singular value decomposition picks the shortest one, through the pseudo-inverse (Chapter 21).
  • Orthogonality works for functions too, with the dot product replaced by an integral, \(\int f(t)g(t)\,dt\). Fourier series are projections onto orthogonal sines and cosines.
  • Proofs of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, that the normal equations give the minimum, and that Gram–Schmidt works: Section C.10.
