14  Span and linear independence

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • describe the span of a set of vectors, and decide whether a vector lies in it
  • test a set of vectors for linear independence, and find the relationship between them when they are dependent
  • connect both ideas to rank and to solving \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\)

Builds on Chapter 2, Chapter 12.

The column picture of Chapter 10 turned every system of equations into a question about vectors: can \(\vect{b}\) be made by combining the columns of \(\M{A}\), and if so, in how many ways? Part III gives these questions names and develops the language for answering them. This chapter introduces the two central ideas. The span of some vectors is everything they can make. They are linearly independent if none of them is wasted: none can be made from the others.

From here on, it helps to name the spaces that vectors live in. \(\R^n\) (said “R n”) stands for the set of all vectors with \(n\) real-number entries. So \(\R^2\) is the plane, \(\R^3\) is ordinary space, and \(\R^n\) for larger \(n\) is the same idea with more entries, even though it can’t be drawn.

14.2 The ideas

Span

A linear combination of vectors \(\vect{v}_1, \dots, \vect{v}_k\) is any vector \[ c_1\vect{v}_1 + c_2\vect{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\vect{v}_k \] with numbers \(c_1, \dots, c_k\), as in Chapter 2. The span of the vectors, written \(\operatorname{span}\{\vect{v}_1, \dots, \vect{v}_k\}\), is the set of all their linear combinations: every point you can reach by scaling them and adding.

In three dimensions the possibilities are:

The vectors Their span
one non-zero vector a line through the origin
two vectors, not along the same line a plane through the origin
three vectors, not all in one plane all of \(\R^3\)

Every span contains the origin (take all the \(c_i = 0\)), so a span is always a line, plane or space through the origin, never one that misses it.

Deciding whether \(\vect{b}\) is in the span is exactly the question of Chapter 10. Put the vectors in the columns of a matrix \(\M{A}\). Then \(\M{A}\vect{c} = c_1\vect{v}_1 + \dots + c_k\vect{v}_k\), so

ImportantSpan and systems

\(\vect{b}\) is in the span of the columns of \(\M{A}\) exactly when \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \vect{b}\) has a solution. The solution gives the coefficients.

To test it, compare ranks (Chapter 12): \(\vect{b}\) is in the span exactly when \(\operatorname{rank}[\M{A} \mid \vect{b}] = \operatorname{rank}\M{A}\).

NoteExample 1: is it in the span?

Let \(\vect{v}_1 = (1, 2, 1)\T\) and \(\vect{v}_2 = (0, 1, 2)\T\). Is \((1, 5, 7)\T\) in their span? Is \((1, 5, 5)\T\)?

Solution. We need \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) with \(c_1\vect{v}_1 + c_2\vect{v}_2 = \vect{b}\). Reduce the augmented matrix for both right-hand sides at once: \[ \amat{rr|rr}{1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 5 & 5 \\ 1 & 2 & 7 & 5} \xrightarrow[R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 - R_1]{R_2 \,\leftarrow\, R_2 - 2R_1} \amat{rr|rr}{1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 & 6 & 4} \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 - 2R_2} \amat{rr|rr}{1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -2}. \] For \((1, 5, 7)\T\) (the first right-hand column) the last row reads \(0 = 0\), so it is in the span, with \(c_1 = 1\) and \(c_2 = 3\). Check: \((1, 2, 1)\T + 3(0, 1, 2)\T = (1, 5, 7)\T\). ✓

For \((1, 5, 5)\T\) the last row reads \(0 = -2\), so it is not in the span. The two vectors span a plane, and \((1, 5, 5)\T\) lies off it.

Linear independence

Some sets of vectors contain a passenger: a vector that is already a combination of the others, and so adds nothing to the span. In Three in a plane, \(\vect{v}_3 = \vect{v}_2 - 2\vect{v}_1\), so \(\vect{v}_3\) lies in the plane that \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\) already span. Moving everything to one side gives a dependence relation, \[ 2\vect{v}_1 - \vect{v}_2 + \vect{v}_3 = \zerovec, \] a combination of the vectors that adds up to zero without all the coefficients being zero.

The vectors \(\vect{v}_1, \dots, \vect{v}_k\) are linearly independent if the only way to combine them to make \(\zerovec\) is the trivial way: \[ c_1\vect{v}_1 + \dots + c_k\vect{v}_k = \zerovec \quad\text{only when}\quad c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0. \] Otherwise they are linearly dependent. The two descriptions match: vectors are dependent exactly when at least one of them is a combination of the others. (If \(c_j \neq 0\) in a dependence relation, divide by \(c_j\) and rearrange to write \(\vect{v}_j\) in terms of the rest.)

Geometrically:

  • two vectors are dependent exactly when they lie along the same line (one is a multiple of the other);
  • three vectors in \(\R^3\) are dependent exactly when they lie in one plane through the origin.

Testing for independence

With the vectors as the columns of \(\M{A}\), a dependence relation is a non-zero solution of \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \zerovec\). So, using Chapter 12:

  • the columns are independent exactly when \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \zerovec\) has only the solution \(\vect{c} = \zerovec\), that is, when there is a pivot in every column: \(\operatorname{rank}\M{A} = k\);
  • if they are dependent, the free variables of \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \zerovec\) give the dependence relations.

For \(n\) vectors in \(\R^n\), \(\M{A}\) is square, and there is a quicker test: the vectors are independent exactly when \(\det\M{A} \neq 0\) (Chapter 6). A non-zero determinant means the unit cube is mapped to a box of non-zero volume, so the columns point in genuinely different directions.

Two facts follow straight away:

  • Any set containing \(\zerovec\) is dependent, because \(1 \cdot \zerovec = \zerovec\) is a dependence relation.
  • More than \(n\) vectors in \(\R^n\) are always dependent. \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \zerovec\) then has more unknowns than equations, so it has non-zero solutions (Chapter 12, Exercise 6). Four vectors in \(\R^3\) can never be independent.
NoteExample 2: finding a dependence relation

Are \(\vect{v}_1 = (1, 0, 1)\T\), \(\vect{v}_2 = (2, 1, 0)\T\) and \(\vect{v}_3 = (0, 1, -2)\T\) independent?

Solution. Solve \(c_1\vect{v}_1 + c_2\vect{v}_2 + c_3\vect{v}_3 = \zerovec\) by reducing the matrix with these columns: \[ \mat{1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & -2} \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 - R_1} \mat{1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 & -2} \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 + 2R_2} \mat{1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0}. \] There is no pivot in column 3, so \(c_3\) is free and the vectors are dependent. Take \(c_3 = 1\). Then row 2 gives \(c_2 = -1\), and row 1 gives \(c_1 = -2c_2 = 2\). So \[ 2\vect{v}_1 - \vect{v}_2 + \vect{v}_3 = \zerovec, \] which you can check entry by entry. The three vectors lie in one plane, the one spanned by \(\vect{v}_1\) and \(\vect{v}_2\). This is the Three in a plane example in the app.

NoteExample 3: three independent vectors

Show that \((1, 1, 0)\T\), \((0, 1, 1)\T\) and \((1, 0, 1)\T\) are independent, and that they span \(\R^3\).

Solution. Three vectors in \(\R^3\) make a square matrix, so use the determinant. Expanding along the first row, \[ \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1(1 - 0) - 0 + 1(1 - 0) = 2 \neq 0. \] So the vectors are independent. And since \(\det\M{A} \neq 0\), \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \vect{b}\) has a solution for every \(\vect{b}\) (Chapter 7), so every vector in \(\R^3\) is in their span.

Why independence matters

If the vectors are independent, every vector in their span can be made from them in exactly one way. For if \[ \vect{b} = c_1\vect{v}_1 + \dots + c_k\vect{v}_k = d_1\vect{v}_1 + \dots + d_k\vect{v}_k, \] then subtracting gives \((c_1 - d_1)\vect{v}_1 + \dots + (c_k - d_k)\vect{v}_k = \zerovec\), and independence forces every \(c_i = d_i\). With dependent vectors, there are always infinitely many ways (the app shows this in Parallel: a line). This is the idea behind coordinates, in Chapter 15.

Subspaces

Spans have a special property. Adding two combinations of \(\vect{v}_1, \dots, \vect{v}_k\) gives another combination of them, and so does multiplying one by a number. A set of vectors with these properties (containing \(\zerovec\), and closed under adding and scaling) is called a subspace. In \(\R^3\), the subspaces are the origin on its own, lines and planes through the origin, and all of \(\R^3\). Every span is a subspace, and every subspace is a span. The subspaces attached to a matrix are the subject of Chapter 16.

14.3 In code

Run this chapter’s code yourself: open the notebook in Colab, or download the Jupyter notebook or the MATLAB script.

Both tests come down to rank. To find a dependence relation, find the solutions of \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \zerovec\): SciPy’s null_space and MATLAB’s null do this. They return a vector of length 1, so divide by one of its entries to get tidier numbers.

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import null_space

v1, v2, v3 = [1, 0, 1], [2, 1, 0], [0, 1, -2]
A = np.column_stack([v1, v2, v3])          # the vectors as columns
print(np.linalg.matrix_rank(A))            # 2 < 3, so dependent

c = null_space(A)                          # solutions of A c = 0
print(c.ravel() / c[2, 0])                 # scaled so that c3 = 1

b = [1, 2, 3]                              # is b in the span?
print(np.linalg.matrix_rank(A), np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.column_stack([A, b])))
2
[ 2. -1.  1.]
2 3

The rank is 2, so the three vectors are dependent, with \(2\vect{v}_1 - \vect{v}_2 + \vect{v}_3 = \zerovec\) as in Example 2. Adding \(\vect{b}\) raises the rank to 3, so \(\vect{b}\) is not in their span.

v1 = [1; 0; 1]; v2 = [2; 1; 0]; v3 = [0; 1; -2];

14.4 Check your understanding

TipWhat is the span of \((1, 2)\T\) and \((-2, -4)\T\)?

The line through the origin in the direction \((1, 2)\T\): the second vector is \(-2\) times the first, so it adds nothing.

TipCan four vectors in \(\R^3\) be linearly independent?

No. More than \(n\) vectors in \(\R^n\) are always dependent.

TipCan a plane that doesn’t pass through the origin be the span of some vectors?

No. Every span contains \(\zerovec\).

14.5 Key ideas

  • The span of a set of vectors is all their linear combinations. \(\vect{b}\) is in the span of the columns of \(\M{A}\) exactly when \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) has a solution.
  • Vectors are linearly independent when no one of them is a combination of the others: the only way to make \(\zerovec\) is with all the amounts zero.
  • To test, put the vectors in the columns of a matrix and row reduce. They are independent exactly when every column has a pivot (the rank equals the number of vectors).
  • A dependence relation can be read from the solutions of \(\M{A}\vect{c} = \zerovec\).
  • A subspace is a set of vectors closed under addition and scaling, such as a line or plane through the origin. Every span is a subspace.

14.7 In practice

  • Redundant sensors: if one sensor’s readings are always a fixed combination of others’ (say, a total flow meter that reads the sum of three branch meters), the measurement vectors are dependent. That redundancy is useful for spotting a faulty sensor, but it adds no new information about the system.
  • Statistics: in regression, if one explanatory variable is (nearly) a combination of the others, the columns of the data matrix are (nearly) dependent. This is called multicollinearity. The fit can still predict well, but the individual coefficients become unreliable, because many combinations explain the data almost equally well.
  • Colour: screens mix red, green and blue light. The three colours are independent, so different mixes give different colours, and together they span the colours the screen can show. A fourth primary would always be a combination of the first three.
  • Structures: the members meeting at a joint of a frame must point in enough independent directions to hold it in place. If they all lie in one plane, the joint can move out of that plane.

14.8 Going further

  • Vectors don’t have to be lists of numbers. Anything that can be added and scaled, following the rules of Chapter 2, forms a vector space: polynomials, functions, signals, images. The functions \(1\), \(x\) and \(x^2\) are linearly independent (no combination of them is zero for every \(x\) unless all the coefficients are zero), and their span is all quadratics. Fourier series write a signal as a combination of the independent functions \(\sin x, \cos x, \sin 2x, \cos 2x, \dots\)
  • Independence is fragile in floating-point arithmetic: a tiny change can turn dependent vectors into independent ones, as the app shows. In practice, the question is often how close to dependent the vectors are, which the singular value decomposition answers (Chapter 21).
  • The largest number of independent vectors in a subspace, and the smallest number that span it, turn out to be the same number: its dimension (Chapter 15).
