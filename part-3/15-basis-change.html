15  Basis, dimension and change of basis

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • recognise a basis, and find a basis and the dimension of a span
  • find the coordinates of a vector in any basis
  • describe a transformation in a new basis using \(\M{P}^{-1}\M{A}\M{P}\), and use this to build matrices from simple pieces

Builds on Chapter 7, Chapter 14.

Coordinates are not built into a vector. They depend on the directions you measure along. Measuring along \(\ui\) and \(\uj\) is natural for a page, but for a reflection it is more natural to measure along the mirror and at right angles to it, and for a rotating body, along its axis. In the right directions, a complicated matrix often becomes very simple. This chapter shows how to choose directions (a basis), how to find a vector’s coordinates in them, and how to rewrite a matrix in them. It is the groundwork for eigenvalues and diagonalisation in Part IV.

15.2 The ideas

Basis

A basis for a subspace (Chapter 14) is a set of vectors that

  1. spans the subspace, so every vector in it can be made from them, and
  2. is linearly independent, so none of them is wasted.

Together these say that every vector in the subspace can be written as a combination of the basis vectors in exactly one way (Chapter 14). The standard basis of \(\R^n\) is \(\ue{1}, \dots, \ue{n}\), the columns of the identity matrix; in \(\R^2\) and \(\R^3\) these are \(\ui\), \(\uj\) and \(\uk\). But there are infinitely many other bases. Any two vectors in \(\R^2\) that don’t lie along the same line form a basis for \(\R^2\).

Dimension

A subspace has many different bases, but they all have the same number of vectors (a proof is in Section C.8). That number is the dimension of the subspace:

  • a line through the origin has dimension 1;
  • a plane through the origin has dimension 2;
  • \(\R^n\) has dimension \(n\), since the standard basis has \(n\) vectors;
  • the subspace containing only \(\zerovec\) has dimension 0, with the empty set as its basis.

So dimension makes the everyday idea precise. It also gives useful shortcuts in \(\R^n\):

  • any \(n\) independent vectors in \(\R^n\) form a basis (they automatically span);
  • any \(n\) vectors that span \(\R^n\) form a basis (they are automatically independent);
  • fewer than \(n\) vectors can’t span \(\R^n\), and more than \(n\) can’t be independent.

Finding a basis for a span

Given some vectors, put them in the columns of a matrix \(\M{A}\) and reduce it to echelon form. The pivot columns tell you which of the vectors to keep: the original vectors in the pivot columns form a basis for their span, and the others are combinations of them. (Row operations change the vectors but not the relationships between them, which is why you go back to the original columns, as in the proof in Section C.6.) So the dimension of the span is the rank of \(\M{A}\).

NoteExample 1: a basis for a span

Find a basis for the span of \((1, 2, 1)\T\), \((2, 4, 2)\T\), \((1, 0, 1)\T\) and \((0, 2, 0)\T\), and its dimension.

Solution. Reduce the matrix with these columns: \[ \mat{1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 0} \xrightarrow[R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 - R_1]{R_2 \,\leftarrow\, R_2 - 2R_1} \mat{1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0}. \] The pivots are in columns 1 and 3, so \((1, 2, 1)\T\) and \((1, 0, 1)\T\) form a basis, and the span has dimension 2: it is a plane. The other two vectors are combinations of these: \((2, 4, 2)\T\) is twice the first, and \((0, 2, 0)\T\) is the first minus the second.

Coordinates

If \(\vect{b}_1, \dots, \vect{b}_n\) is a basis for \(\R^n\), each vector \(\vect{v}\) can be written in exactly one way as \[ \vect{v} = c_1\vect{b}_1 + \dots + c_n\vect{b}_n. \] The numbers \(c_1, \dots, c_n\) are the coordinates of \(\vect{v}\) in this basis, written \([\vect{v}]_B\). The usual entries of \(\vect{v}\) are just its coordinates in the standard basis.

Put the basis vectors in the columns of a matrix \(\M{P}\). By the column view of multiplication, \[ \vect{v} = \M{P}\,[\vect{v}]_B \qquad\text{and so}\qquad [\vect{v}]_B = \M{P}^{-1}\vect{v}. \tag{15.1}\] \(\M{P}\) is invertible because its columns are independent. Multiplying by \(\M{P}\) converts new coordinates to standard ones; multiplying by \(\M{P}^{-1}\), or better, solving \(\M{P}\vect{c} = \vect{v}\), converts the other way.

NoteExample 2: coordinates in a new basis

Show that \(\vect{b}_1 = (1, 1)\T\) and \(\vect{b}_2 = (1, -1)\T\) form a basis for \(\R^2\), and find the coordinates of \(\vect{v} = (5, 1)\T\) in it.

Solution. The vectors don’t lie along the same line (\(\det\mat{1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1} = -2 \neq 0\)), so they are independent, and two independent vectors in \(\R^2\) form a basis. Solve \(c_1\vect{b}_1 + c_2\vect{b}_2 = \vect{v}\): \[ \begin{aligned} c_1 + c_2 &= 5 \\ c_1 - c_2 &= 1 \end{aligned} \] Adding gives \(2c_1 = 6\), so \(c_1 = 3\) and \(c_2 = 2\): \([\vect{v}]_B = (3, 2)\T\). Check: \(3(1, 1)\T + 2(1, -1)\T = (5, 1)\T\). ✓ This is Example 2 in the app.

A transformation in a new basis

Suppose \(\M{A}\) is the matrix of a transformation in standard coordinates. What is its matrix in the basis \(B\)? Start with a vector’s new coordinates \([\vect{x}]_B\), and follow three steps, each a matrix multiplication:

  1. convert to standard coordinates: multiply by \(\M{P}\);
  2. apply the transformation: multiply by \(\M{A}\);
  3. convert back to new coordinates: multiply by \(\M{P}^{-1}\).

So the matrix of the same transformation in the new basis is \[ \M{B} = \M{P}^{-1}\M{A}\M{P}. \tag{15.2}\] Read it from right to left, as with any composition (Chapter 5). Turned round, \(\M{A} = \M{P}\M{B}\M{P}^{-1}\): this lets you build a matrix by describing what it does in a convenient basis, as Example 3 shows.

Two matrices related by \(\M{B} = \M{P}^{-1}\M{A}\M{P}\) for some invertible \(\M{P}\) are called similar. Similar matrices describe the same transformation in different coordinates, so they share everything that doesn’t depend on the coordinates. For example, they have the same determinant (the same area or volume scale factor): \[ \det\M{B} = \det\M{P}^{-1}\,\det\M{A}\,\det\M{P} = \det\M{A}. \] They also have the same eigenvalues (Chapter 18).

NoteExample 3: a reflection built in a convenient basis

Find the matrix of the reflection in the line \(y = 2x\).

Solution. In the right basis, this reflection is easy to describe. Take \(\vect{b}_1 = (1, 2)\T\) along the mirror and \(\vect{b}_2 = (-2, 1)\T\) at right angles to it (Figure 15.1). The reflection leaves \(\vect{b}_1\) alone and reverses \(\vect{b}_2\), so in this basis its matrix is \[ \M{B} = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1}. \] With \(\M{P} = \mat{1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1}\), whose determinant is 5, the inverse is \(\M{P}^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{5}\mat{1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1}\), and so \[ \M{A} = \M{P}\M{B}\M{P}^{-1} = \mat{1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1} \cdot \frac{1}{5}\mat{1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1} = \frac{1}{5}\mat{-3 & 4 \\ 4 & 3}. \] Check: \(\M{A}(1, 2)\T = \tfrac{1}{5}(5, 10)\T = (1, 2)\T\), so the mirror line is fixed. ✓ This agrees with the reflection formula in Chapter 4.

NoteExample 4: a matrix that becomes diagonal

In Chapter 9, the matrix \(\M{A} = \mat{3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2}\) had invariant lines along \((1, 1)\T\), stretched by 4, and \((1, -2)\T\), fixed. Find its matrix in the basis of these two vectors.

Solution. With \(\M{P} = \mat{1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2}\), first compute \(\M{A}\M{P}\), whose columns are the images of the basis vectors: \[ \M{A}\M{P} = \mat{4 & 1 \\ 4 & -2}. \] Column 1 is \(4\vect{b}_1\) and column 2 is \(1\vect{b}_2\), so in the new basis the images have coordinates \((4, 0)\T\) and \((0, 1)\T\): \[ \M{P}^{-1}\M{A}\M{P} = \mat{4 & 0 \\ 0 & 1}. \] In the basis of its invariant lines, the matrix is diagonal: it just stretches each new coordinate. This is the idea behind diagonalisation (Chapter 19), and the Invariant lines preset in the app shows this basis.

15.3 In code

Coordinates come from solving \(\M{P}\vect{c} = \vect{v}\), and the change of basis from solving \(\M{P}\M{B} = \M{A}\M{P}\). As usual, it is better to solve than to compute \(\M{P}^{-1}\) (Chapter 7).

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

P = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]])            # basis vectors as columns
v = np.array([5, 1])
print(np.linalg.solve(P, v))               # coordinates of v (Example 2)

A = np.array([[3, 1], [2, 2]])
P = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -2]])
B = np.linalg.solve(P, A @ P)              # P^(-1) A P (Example 4)
print(np.round(B, 10) + 0)

15.4 Check your understanding

TipDo \((1, 0, 0)\T\) and \((0, 1, 0)\T\) form a basis for \(\R^3\)?

No: two vectors can’t span \(\R^3\), which has dimension 3. They form a basis for the \(xy\)-plane.

TipIf \(\vect{b}_1\) is doubled in length, what happens to the first coordinate of every vector?

It halves: you need half as many of the longer steps.

TipCan two similar matrices have different determinants?

No: similar matrices describe the same transformation, and \(\det(\M{P}^{-1}\M{A}\M{P}) = \det\M{A}\).

15.5 Key ideas

  • A basis is a set of independent vectors that spans a space. Every vector in the space can then be written as a combination of them in exactly one way.
  • Every basis of a space has the same number of vectors, its dimension.
  • The pivot columns of a matrix form a basis for the span of its columns.
  • The coordinates of \(\vect{x}\) in the basis that makes up the columns of \(\M{P}\) are \(\M{P}^{-1}\vect{x}\).
  • In that basis, the transformation \(\M{A}\) has the matrix \(\M{P}^{-1}\M{A}\M{P}\). Choosing a convenient basis can make a matrix simple, even diagonal.

15.7 In practice

  • Finite element analysis: each beam or panel in a structure is easiest to describe in its own local coordinates, along and across the member. The program converts each member’s stiffness matrix to the global coordinates of the whole structure with a change of basis, \(\M{P}\M{K}\M{P}\T\), before assembling them.
  • Robotics and graphics: a robot arm or an animated character is a chain of parts, each with its own coordinate frame. Positions are passed along the chain by multiplying change-of-basis matrices (Chapter 34).
  • Crystals and materials: properties such as stiffness or electrical conductivity are simplest along a crystal’s own axes, and are converted to laboratory axes by a change of basis.
  • Data: principal component analysis (Chapter 21) finds a new basis in which a cloud of data points is described most efficiently.

15.8 Going further

  • Bases exist for any vector space, not just \(\R^n\). The polynomials of degree at most 2 have the basis \(1, x, x^2\), so they form a space of dimension 3. Some spaces, such as the space of all continuous functions, have no finite basis at all, and are infinite-dimensional.
  • A transformation between abstract vector spaces, such as differentiation of polynomials, has a matrix once a basis is chosen for each space, and changing the bases changes the matrix as in Equation 15.2.
  • Proof that every basis of a subspace has the same number of vectors: Section C.8.
