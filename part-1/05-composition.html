5  Composing transformations

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • find the single matrix for “do \(\M{B}\), then \(\M{A}\)” as the product \(\M{A}\M{B}\)
  • explain geometrically why \(\M{A}\M{B}\) and \(\M{B}\M{A}\) usually differ
  • use composition to derive results such as the angle addition formulas
  • break a transformation into simpler steps

Builds on Chapter 3, Chapter 4.

Chapter 3 defined matrix multiplication by the row-times-column rule and promised a reason for it. Here is the reason: multiplying matrices is doing one transformation after another. The strange-looking rule is exactly what makes this work.

5.1 Build intuition

Take a quarter turn anticlockwise, \(\M{R} = \mat{0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0}\), and the reflection in the \(x\)-axis, \(\M{X} = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1}\). The app below applies them to a letter F in both orders: on the left the rotation first, on the right the reflection first. The dotted outline shows the F after the first step.

Open the composition explorer in its own tab

The two orders give different results. Rotating and then reflecting gives the reflection in the line \(y = -x\); reflecting and then rotating gives the reflection in \(y = x\).

Things to try:

  • Choose other pairs for \(\M{A}\) and \(\M{B}\). Find a pair that gives the same result in both orders, and a pair that doesn’t.
  • Make \(\M{A}\) and \(\M{B}\) both rotations. Does the order ever matter?
  • Look at the matrices printed under each picture, and check one of them by multiplying.

5.2 The ideas

Doing one thing after another

Suppose we apply \(\M{B}\) to a vector \(\vect{x}\), then apply \(\M{A}\) to the result. The final position is \(\M{A}(\M{B}\vect{x})\). By associativity (Chapter 3) this equals \((\M{A}\M{B})\vect{x}\), so:

ImportantComposition is multiplication

“Apply \(\M{B}\), then \(\M{A}\)” is the single transformation with matrix \(\M{A}\M{B}\).

Notice the order: the transformation done first is written on the right, nearest to \(\vect{x}\). This matches function notation, where \(f(g(x))\) means “do \(g\), then \(f\)”. A sequence \(\M{A}_1\), then \(\M{A}_2\), then \(\M{A}_3\) has matrix \(\M{A}_3\M{A}_2\M{A}_1\).

You can also see why the row-times-column rule had to be the definition. Column 1 of the combined matrix must be where \(\ui\) ends up (Chapter 4). First \(\M{B}\) sends \(\ui\) to column 1 of \(\M{B}\); then \(\M{A}\) sends that to \(\M{A}\) times column 1 of \(\M{B}\). That is exactly column 1 of \(\M{A}\M{B}\), by the column view of multiplication (Chapter 3). The same goes for \(\uj\) and column 2.

NoteExample 1: a sequence of three steps

Find the single matrix for: stretch by factor 2 parallel to the \(x\)-axis, then rotate by \(90°\) anticlockwise, then reflect in the \(x\)-axis. Where does the point \((1, 1)\) end up?

Solution. The matrices, in the order applied, are \[ \M{S} = \mat{2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1}, \] \[ \M{R} = \mat{0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0}, \] \[ \M{X} = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1}. \] The combined matrix is \(\M{X}\M{R}\M{S}\): the first step on the right. \[ \M{R}\M{S} = \mat{0 & -1 \\ 2 & 0}, \] \[ \M{X}\M{R}\M{S} = \mat{0 & -1 \\ -2 & 0}. \] So \((1, 1)\) goes to \(\M{X}\M{R}\M{S}\,(1, 1)\T = (-1, -2)\T\). Check step by step: \((1, 1) \to (2, 1) \to (-1, 2) \to (-1, -2)\). ✓

NoteExample 2: undoing a transformation by composing

Find a matrix \(\M{B}\) such that applying the shear \(\M{H} = \mat{1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1}\) and then \(\M{B}\) brings every point back to where it started.

Solution. The shear moves each point sideways by 3 times its height, without changing its height. To undo it, move each point back by 3 times its height: \(\M{B} = \mat{1 & -3 \\ 0 & 1}\). Check: \(\M{B}\M{H} = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1} = \M{I}\). This is an example of an inverse matrix, the subject of Chapter 7.

Order matters

Since \(\M{A}\M{B}\) and \(\M{B}\M{A}\) are different transformations, there is no reason for them to be equal, and usually they are not. In the example above, \[ \M{X}\M{R} = \mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1}\mat{0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0} = \mat{0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0}, \] \[ \M{R}\M{X} = \mat{0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0}\mat{1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1} = \mat{0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0}. \] These are the reflections in \(y = -x\) and \(y = x\), as the app showed.

Some pairs do commute, and it helps to know which:

  • two rotations about the origin: turning by \(\alpha\) then \(\beta\) is the same as \(\beta\) then \(\alpha\);
  • two stretches along the same axes (two diagonal matrices);
  • any matrix with an enlargement \(k\M{I}\), or with the identity;
  • any matrix with its own powers.
NoteExample 3: does this pair commute?

Does the shear \(\M{H} = \mat{1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1}\) commute with the stretch \(\M{S} = \mat{2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1}\)?

Solution. \[ \M{H}\M{S} = \mat{2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1}, \] \[ \M{S}\M{H} = \mat{2 & 2 \\ 0 & 1}. \] They differ, so no. Geometrically: stretching first and then shearing slides the top of the unit square by 1; shearing first and then stretching doubles that slide to 2.

Two rotations: the angle addition formulas

Rotating by \(\beta\) and then by \(\alpha\) must be a rotation by \(\alpha + \beta\), so \(\M{R}(\alpha)\M{R}(\beta) = \M{R}(\alpha + \beta)\). Multiplying out the left side, \[ \mat{\cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha}\mat{\cos\beta & -\sin\beta \\ \sin\beta & \cos\beta} = \mat{\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta & \cdots \\ \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta & \cdots}. \] Comparing the first column with that of \(\M{R}(\alpha + \beta)\) gives both angle addition formulas at once: \[ \cos(\alpha + \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta, \] \[ \sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta. \] It also follows that \(\M{R}(\theta)^n = \M{R}(n\theta)\). For example, \(\M{R}(60°)^6 = \M{R}(360°) = \M{I}\).

Two reflections make a rotation

Reflecting in a line through the origin at angle \(\varphi\), then in a line at angle \(\theta\), gives a rotation by \(2(\theta - \varphi)\), twice the angle between the lines (Figure 5.1). (Exercise 4 asks you to check this by multiplying, using the optional reflection formula in Chapter 4.) You can see it with a pair of mirrors: two reflections reverse the picture twice, so the result is the right way round, but turned.

Breaking a transformation into pieces

Composition also works backwards: a complicated matrix can often be understood as a sequence of simple ones. For example, \[ \mat{1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1} = \sqrt{2}\,\mat{\cos 45° & -\sin 45° \\ \sin 45° & \cos 45°}, \] so it is a rotation by \(45°\) followed by an enlargement with factor \(\sqrt{2}\) (in either order, because an enlargement gives the same result whether it is done first or second). Every matrix of the form \(\mat{a & -b \\ b & a}\) is a rotation combined with an enlargement in this way.

5.3 In code

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

def R(theta_deg):
    """Rotation matrix for an anticlockwise rotation by theta degrees."""
    t = np.radians(theta_deg)
    return np.array([[np.cos(t), -np.sin(t)],
                     [np.sin(t),  np.cos(t)]])

print(np.allclose(R(25) @ R(50), R(75)))          # rotations add
print(np.allclose(np.linalg.matrix_power(R(60), 6), np.eye(2)))

S = np.array([[2, 0], [0, 1]])
X = np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]])
M = X @ R(90) @ S                                  # S first, then R, then X
print(np.round(M, 10))
print(M @ np.array([1, 1]))

5.4 Check your understanding

TipYou want to apply \(\M{A}\), then \(\M{B}\), then \(\M{C}\) to a vector. Which single matrix does this?

\(\M{C}\M{B}\M{A}\): the first transformation goes on the right.

TipWhat single transformation is a rotation by \(150°\) followed by a rotation by \(210°\)?

A rotation by \(360°\), which is the identity.

TipTwo reflections are in lines at \(10°\) and \(40°\) to the \(x\)-axis. Reflecting in the \(10°\) line first, what is the combined transformation?

A rotation by \(2(40° - 10°) = 60°\) anticlockwise.

5.5 Key ideas

  • Doing \(\M{B}\) and then \(\M{A}\) is the single transformation \(\M{A}\M{B}\): the matrix applied first is written on the right.
  • \(\M{A}\M{B}\) and \(\M{B}\M{A}\) are usually different transformations, which is the geometric reason why matrix multiplication isn’t commutative.
  • Composing two rotations adds their angles, and multiplying the matrices gives the angle addition formulas.
  • Two reflections make a rotation, and many transformations can be broken into simpler steps, such as a rotation and a stretch.

5.7 In practice

  • Robot arms compose one rotation per joint. The position of the gripper is the product of all the joint matrices applied to the tool’s position (Chapter 34).
  • Computer graphics builds a single matrix from a chain of steps (place the model in the world, move to the camera’s viewpoint, project onto the screen) and applies it to every vertex.
  • Aircraft and spacecraft describe which way they are facing by three turns applied in a fixed order: yaw (turning left or right), pitch (nose up or down) and roll (tilting the wings). Using the wrong order is a classic source of navigation errors (Chapter 8).
  • Optics: the path of a light ray through a camera lens system is tracked by multiplying one small matrix for each lens and each gap, in the order the light meets them.

5.8 Going further

  • Matrices of the form \(\mat{a & -b \\ b & a}\) multiply exactly like the complex numbers \(a + bi\). Multiplying by a complex number rotates and enlarges, which is why complex numbers are so useful for waves and alternating current.
  • Every \(2 \times 2\) matrix can be written as a rotation (or reflection), then a stretch along the axes, then another rotation (or reflection). This is the singular value decomposition of Chapter 21, one of the most useful results in applied mathematics.
  • Rotations about the origin form what mathematicians call a group: combining two rotations gives a rotation, every rotation can be undone by another, and doing nothing counts as a rotation. Groups of transformations describe the symmetries of crystals, molecules and the laws of physics.
