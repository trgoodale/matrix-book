7  Inverse matrices

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • find the inverse of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, and check it
  • interpret the inverse as the transformation that undoes \(\M{A}\)
  • use the rules \((\M{A}\M{B})^{-1} = \M{B}^{-1}\M{A}^{-1}\) and \((\M{A}\T)^{-1} = (\M{A}^{-1})\T\)
  • find a \(3 \times 3\) inverse with cofactors
  • explain why singular matrices have no inverse, and why software rarely computes inverses

Builds on Chapter 5, Chapter 6.

For numbers, dividing by \(a\) is multiplying by \(a^{-1}\), the number with \(a^{-1}a = 1\). Matrices have no division, but many square matrices have an inverse that plays the same role. Geometrically, the inverse is the transformation that undoes \(\M{A}\): it puts every point back where it came from.

7.1 Build intuition

The app below applies a matrix to a letter F and then tries to undo it by applying the inverse matrix. Choose a transformation, press Apply M, then Undo.

Open the undo explorer in its own tab

Think about which transformations from Chapter 4 can be undone:

  • A rotation by \(\theta\) is undone by a rotation by \(-\theta\).
  • A reflection is undone by doing the same reflection again.
  • A shear with factor \(k\) is undone by a shear with factor \(-k\).
  • An enlargement by 3 is undone by an enlargement by \(\tfrac{1}{3}\).
  • A projection onto the \(x\)-axis cannot be undone. The points \((2, 0)\), \((2, 1)\) and \((2, -5)\) all land on \((2, 0)\), so given only \((2, 0)\), there is no way to know where it started.

The projection is the one with determinant zero. That is not a coincidence. Try Project onto x-axis and Squash flat in the app: the app marks two different starting points that end up in the same place.

7.2 The ideas

Definition

A square matrix \(\M{A}\) is invertible if there is a matrix \(\M{A}^{-1}\) with \[ \M{A}^{-1}\M{A} = \M{I} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \M{A}\M{A}^{-1} = \M{I}. \] The first equation says \(\M{A}^{-1}\) undoes \(\M{A}\); the second says \(\M{A}\) undoes \(\M{A}^{-1}\). For square matrices, either equation implies the other. An invertible matrix has only one inverse (see Section C.3). A matrix with no inverse is singular.

Only square matrices have inverses. A \(3 \times 2\) matrix, for example, maps the plane into three-dimensional space, and can’t fill it; there is no way back from points it misses.

The \(2 \times 2\) inverse

For \(\M{A} = \mat{a & b \\ c & d}\) with \(\det\M{A} = ad - bc \neq 0\), \[ \M{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\mat{d & -b \\ -c & a}. \tag{7.1}\]

In words: swap the diagonal entries, negate the off-diagonal entries, and divide by the determinant. You can check it by multiplying: \[ \mat{a & b \\ c & d}\mat{d & -b \\ -c & a} = \mat{ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & ad - bc} = (ad - bc)\M{I}. \]

NoteExample 1: a \(2 \times 2\) inverse

Find the inverse of \(\M{A} = \mat{4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6}\), and use it to solve \(4x + 7y = 1\), \(2x + 6y = 2\).

Solution. \(\det\M{A} = 24 - 14 = 10\), so \[ \M{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}\mat{6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4} = \mat{0.6 & -0.7 \\ -0.2 & 0.4}. \] Check: \[\M{A}\M{A}^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{10}\mat{24 - 14 & -28 + 28 \\ 12 - 12 & -14 + 24} = \M{I}. \;✓\]

The system is \(\M{A}\vect{x} = (1, 2)\T\), so \[ \vect{x} = \M{A}^{-1}\mat{1 \\ 2} = \mat{0.6 - 1.4 \\ -0.2 + 0.8} = \mat{-0.8 \\ 0.6}. \]

NoteExample 2: the inverse of a rotation

Show that the inverse of the rotation \(\M{R}(\theta)\) is \(\M{R}(-\theta)\), and that it equals \(\M{R}(\theta)\T\).

Solution. The determinant is \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\), so by Equation 7.1, \[ \M{R}(\theta)^{-1} = \mat{\cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta} = \mat{\cos(-\theta) & -\sin(-\theta) \\ \sin(-\theta) & \cos(-\theta)} = \M{R}(-\theta), \] which is also the transpose of \(\M{R}(\theta)\). Matrices whose inverse is their transpose are called orthogonal. They are exactly the transformations that preserve lengths and angles, and they make many calculations easy (Chapter 17).

Singular matrices

If \(\det\M{A} = 0\), the formula would divide by zero, and in fact no inverse exists. Geometrically, \(\M{A}\) squashes the plane onto a line (or a point), so different points land in the same place and the transformation can’t be reversed. Putting together this chapter and the last:

ImportantInvertible matrices

For a square matrix \(\M{A}\), the following are equivalent:

  • \(\M{A}\) has an inverse;
  • \(\det\M{A} \neq 0\);
  • \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) has exactly one solution for every \(\vect{b}\), namely \(\vect{x} = \M{A}^{-1}\vect{b}\);
  • \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \zerovec\) has only the solution \(\vect{x} = \zerovec\).

The list grows as the book goes on; Chapter 16 gives the full version.

Rules for inverses

Rule Why
\((\M{A}\M{B})^{-1} = \M{B}^{-1}\M{A}^{-1}\) To undo “\(\M{B}\) then \(\M{A}\)”, undo \(\M{A}\) first, then \(\M{B}\)
\(\det(\M{A}^{-1}) = 1/\det\M{A}\) Undoing a scaling divides by the same factor
\((\M{A}\T)^{-1} = (\M{A}^{-1})\T\) Transpose both sides of \(\M{A}\M{A}^{-1} = \M{I}\)

A few more are listed in Appendix B.

WarningWatch out: order and sides

The order in \((\M{A}\M{B})^{-1} = \M{B}^{-1}\M{A}^{-1}\) is reversed. This is sometimes called the socks and shoes rule: you put on socks, then shoes, but take off shoes, then socks. It is proved in Section C.3.

Inverses also let you cancel, but only from the same side. If \(\M{A}\) is invertible and \(\M{A}\M{B} = \M{A}\M{C}\), multiplying both sides on the left by \(\M{A}^{-1}\) gives \(\M{B} = \M{C}\). But \(\M{A}\M{B} = \M{C}\M{A}\) does not give \(\M{B} = \M{C}\). Without invertibility, cancelling fails altogether, as Chapter 3 showed.

Larger inverses: cofactors

For an \(n \times n\) matrix, the cofactor \(C_{ij}\) is the minor of entry \(a_{ij}\) (the determinant left after crossing out row \(i\) and column \(j\)) with the checkerboard sign \((-1)^{i+j}\), as in Chapter 6. The adjugate of \(\M{A}\) is the transpose of the matrix of cofactors, and \[ \M{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det\M{A}}\operatorname{adj}\M{A}. \] For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix this gives Equation 7.1. For \(3 \times 3\) it works well by hand (see Example 3). For larger matrices it is far too slow; Chapter 12 finds inverses by row reduction instead.

NoteExample 3: a \(3 \times 3\) inverse by cofactors

Find the inverse of \(\M{A} = \mat{1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0}\).

Solution. Expanding along the first row, \[\det\M{A} = 1(0 - 24) - 2(0 - 20) + 3(0 - 5) = 1.\]

The cofactors, with signs, are \[ \begin{aligned} C_{11} &= +(0 - 24) = -24, & C_{12} &= -(0 - 20) = 20, & C_{13} &= +(0 - 5) = -5, \\ C_{21} &= -(0 - 18) = 18, & C_{22} &= +(0 - 15) = -15, & C_{23} &= -(6 - 10) = 4, \\ C_{31} &= +(8 - 3) = 5, & C_{32} &= -(4 - 0) = -4, & C_{33} &= +(1 - 0) = 1. \end{aligned} \] The adjugate is the transpose of the cofactor matrix, and \(\det\M{A} = 1\), so \[ \M{A}^{-1} = \mat{-24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1}. \] Check one entry of \(\M{A}\M{A}^{-1}\): row 1 of \(\M{A}\) times column 1 of \(\M{A}^{-1}\) is \(-24 + 40 - 15 = 1\). ✓

Solving systems with inverses (and why software doesn’t)

If \(\M{A}\) is invertible, multiplying \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) on the left by \(\M{A}^{-1}\) gives \[ \vect{x} = \M{A}^{-1}\vect{b}. \] This is a neat way to write the solution, and for \(2 \times 2\) systems it is a quick way to find it. For anything larger, computing the inverse and then multiplying is slower and less accurate than solving the system directly by elimination (Chapter 11). Numerical software follows this rule, and so should you. When you see \(\M{A}^{-1}\vect{b}\) in a formula, read it as “the solution of \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\)”.

NoteExample 4: solving a matrix equation

\(\M{A}\) and \(\M{B}\) are invertible. Solve \(\M{A}\M{X}\M{B} = \M{C}\) for \(\M{X}\).

Solution. Multiply on the left by \(\M{A}^{-1}\) and on the right by \(\M{B}^{-1}\): \[ \M{A}^{-1}\M{A}\M{X}\M{B}\M{B}^{-1} = \M{A}^{-1}\M{C}\M{B}^{-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \M{X} = \M{A}^{-1}\M{C}\M{B}^{-1}. \] Writing \(\M{X} = \M{C}\M{A}^{-1}\M{B}^{-1}\), or anything else in a different order, would be wrong.

7.3 In code

Run this chapter’s code yourself: open the notebook in Colab, or download the Jupyter notebook or the MATLAB script.

  • Python
  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[4, 7], [2, 6]])
b = np.array([1, 2])

print(np.linalg.inv(A))          # the inverse, if you really need it
print(np.linalg.solve(A, b))     # the right way to solve Ax = b
[[ 0.6 -0.7]
 [-0.2  0.4]]
[-0.8  0.6]

The difference matters for large systems. Here both approaches solve a random \(2000 \times 2000\) system, and we compare the time taken and the size of the error, measured by how far \(\M{A}\vect{x}\) is from \(\vect{b}\):

import time
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n = 2000
M = rng.standard_normal((n, n))
v = rng.standard_normal(n)

t0 = time.perf_counter(); x1 = np.linalg.solve(M, v); t1 = time.perf_counter()
x2 = np.linalg.inv(M) @ v;                             t2 = time.perf_counter()
print(f"solve: {t1 - t0:.3f} s, error {np.linalg.norm(M @ x1 - v):.1e}")
print(f"inv:   {t2 - t1:.3f} s, error {np.linalg.norm(M @ x2 - v):.1e}")
solve: 1.950 s, error 6.0e-11
inv:   1.647 s, error 3.5e-10

n = 2000;

Using the inverse typically takes several times as long and gives a larger error. MATLAB’s code editor warns you when it sees inv(A)*b for this reason.

7.4 Check your understanding

TipWhat is the inverse of \(\mat{2 & 0 \\ 0 & 5}\)?

\(\mat{1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/5}\). Undoing a stretch divides by the same factors; for any diagonal matrix with non-zero diagonal entries, invert each one.

TipDoes \(\mat{3 & 6 \\ 1 & 2}\) have an inverse?

No: its determinant is \(6 - 6 = 0\). Its columns are parallel, so it squashes the plane onto the line through \((3, 1)\).

TipIf \(\M{A}^2 = \M{I}\), what is \(\M{A}^{-1}\)? Give a geometric example.

\(\M{A}^{-1} = \M{A}\), since \(\M{A}\M{A} = \M{I}\). Any reflection is an example: reflecting twice gets you back to the start.

7.5 Key ideas

  • The inverse \(\M{A}^{-1}\) undoes \(\M{A}\): \(\M{A}^{-1}\M{A} = \M{A}\M{A}^{-1} = \M{I}\).
  • For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, swap the diagonal entries, change the signs of the others, and divide by the determinant. Always check by multiplying back.
  • A matrix has an inverse exactly when \(\det\M{A} \neq 0\). A singular matrix loses information, so nothing can undo it.
  • \((\M{A}\M{B})^{-1} = \M{B}^{-1}\M{A}^{-1}\): to undo two steps, undo the last one first.
  • Software solves \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) by elimination, not by forming \(\M{A}^{-1}\), which is slower and less accurate.

  7. Code. The Hill cipher is a simple secret code. It turns letters into numbers (\(A = 0, B = 1, \dots, Z = 25\)), multiplies pairs of them by a key matrix, and then replaces each result by its remainder after dividing by 26 (“reducing modulo 26”), so that it is a letter again. With key \(\M{K} = \mat{3 & 3 \\ 2 & 5}\), the message HELP becomes the vectors \((7, 4)\T\) and \((11, 15)\T\). Encrypt it. 7.7 In practice

  • Coordinate frames: if \(\M{A}\) converts from a robot’s own coordinates to the room’s, then \(\M{A}^{-1}\) converts back. For rotations this is just the transpose, which is cheap and exact.
  • Formulas: inverses appear throughout statistics and engineering. For example, the best straight line through a set of data points is given by a formula containing \((\M{X}\T\M{X})^{-1}\) (Chapter 17). Formulas like this state the answer neatly, but software computes them by solving systems instead.
  • Cryptography: decrypting a Hill cipher means multiplying by the inverse of the key matrix (Chapter 39).
