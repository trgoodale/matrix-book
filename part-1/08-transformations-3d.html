8  Transformations in three dimensions

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • write \(3 \times 3\) matrices for rotations about the coordinate axes, reflections in coordinate planes, stretches and projections
  • use the right-hand rule to get the signs of rotation matrices right
  • recognise a rotation from its matrix, and see why the order of rotations matters

Builds on Chapter 4, Chapter 5.

Everything in the last four chapters carries over to three dimensions. A \(3 \times 3\) matrix moves every point of space, its columns are the images of \(\ui\), \(\uj\) and \(\uk\), composition is multiplication, and the determinant is a volume scale factor. This chapter collects the standard transformations. Chapter 9 then asks what a transformation leaves unchanged.

8.2 The ideas

Rotations about the axes

A rotation about the \(z\)-axis leaves \(z\)-coordinates alone and rotates the \(xy\)-plane exactly as in two dimensions. Rotations about the other axes are similar: \[ \M{R}_x(\theta) = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta}, \] \[ \M{R}_y(\theta) = \mat{\cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta}, \] \[ \M{R}_z(\theta) = \mat{\cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}. \]

The right-hand rule (as for the cross product in the Refresher) fixes which way is positive: point your right thumb along the positive axis, and your fingers curl in the direction of a positive rotation. So a positive rotation about \(z\) takes \(x\) towards \(y\); about \(x\), it takes \(y\) towards \(z\); and about \(y\), it takes \(z\) towards \(x\) (Figure 8.1).

That last one explains the odd-looking signs in \(\M{R}_y\). A positive rotation about \(y\) moves \(\uk\) towards \(\ui\), so the image of \(\uk\) (column 3) has a positive \(x\)-component, \(\sin\theta\), and the image of \(\ui\) has a negative \(z\)-component. If you list the axes in the cyclic order \(z, x\) rather than \(x, z\), it looks just like the others.

All three are orthogonal matrices (Chapter 7): \(\M{R}\T\M{R} = \M{I}\), and their determinant is 1. This works the other way round too. Any \(3 \times 3\) orthogonal matrix with determinant 1 is a rotation about some axis through the origin (a proof is in Section C.4). Chapter 9 shows how to find the axis.

NoteExample 1: a rotation about the \(z\)-axis

Find the image of \((1, 2, 3)\) under a rotation by \(90°\) about the \(z\)-axis.

Solution. With \(\theta = 90°\), \[ \M{R}_z(90°)\mat{1 \\ 2 \\ 3} = \mat{0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}\mat{1 \\ 2 \\ 3} = \mat{-2 \\ 1 \\ 3}. \] The height \(z = 3\) is unchanged, and \((1, 2)\) has turned a quarter turn to \((-2, 1)\).

NoteExample 2: order matters in three dimensions

Show that rotating by \(90°\) about the \(x\)-axis and then by \(90°\) about the \(z\)-axis is not the same as doing them in the other order.

Solution. \[ \M{R}_z(90°)\M{R}_x(90°) = \mat{0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}\mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0} = \mat{0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0}, \] \[ \M{R}_x(90°)\M{R}_z(90°) = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0}\mat{0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1} = \mat{0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0}. \] They differ. (The first product turns out to be a single rotation, by \(120°\) about the line through the origin and \((1, 1, 1)\): see Figure 9.2 in the next chapter.) In two dimensions all rotations about the origin commute; in three dimensions, rotations about different axes usually don’t. Try it with a book: turn it a quarter turn about one edge, then about another, then start again in the other order.

Reflections, stretches and projections

Transformation Matrix
Reflection in the plane \(z = 0\) (the \(xy\)-plane) \(\mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1}\)
Reflection in the plane \(x = y\) \(\mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}\)
Stretch by factors \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) along the axes \(\mat{a & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & b & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & c}\)
Projection onto the \(xy\)-plane \(\mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0}\)
Shear: \(x\) increases by \(k\) times the height \(z\) \(\mat{1 & 0 & k \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}\)

As always, each column is the image of a unit vector. Reflections have determinant \(-1\), projections have determinant 0, and shears have determinant 1.

8.3 In code

  • MATLAB
import numpy as np

def Rx(t):
    c, s = np.cos(t), np.sin(t)
    return np.array([[1, 0, 0], [0, c, -s], [0, s, c]])

def Rz(t):
    c, s = np.cos(t), np.sin(t)
    return np.array([[c, -s, 0], [s, c, 0], [0, 0, 1]])

q = np.pi / 2
print(np.round(Rz(q) @ Rx(q), 10))
print(np.round(Rx(q) @ Rz(q), 10))

# Check that a rotation is orthogonal with determinant 1
R = Rz(q) @ Rx(q)
print(np.allclose(R.T @ R, np.eye(3)), np.linalg.det(R))

clean rounds to 10 decimal places, which removes tiny errors such as \(\cos(\pi/2) \approx 6 \times 10^{-17}\). The check uses norm, which measures the size of a matrix, so a tiny value means \(\M{R}\T\M{R}\) is \(\M{I}\) up to rounding error; 1 means true.

8.4 Check your understanding

TipWhat is the matrix of the reflection in the plane \(y = 0\)?

\(\mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}\): only the \(y\)-coordinate changes sign.

TipWhy is \(\M{R}_x(\theta)^{-1} = \M{R}_x(-\theta)\)?

Undoing a rotation by \(\theta\) means rotating by \(-\theta\) about the same axis. Since \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\) and \(\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta\), \(\M{R}_x(-\theta)\) is also the transpose of \(\M{R}_x(\theta)\), as it must be for an orthogonal matrix.

TipWhat is the determinant of the projection onto the \(xy\)-plane, and why?

0: it squashes every solid flat, so every volume becomes 0.

8.5 Key ideas

  • A \(3 \times 3\) matrix transforms space, and its columns are where \(\ui\), \(\uj\) and \(\uk\) land.
  • Rotations about the coordinate axes have a \(2 \times 2\) rotation block and a 1 on the diagonal for the axis that stays fixed.
  • The right-hand rule fixes the direction of a positive rotation, and so the signs of the sines.
  • A rotation matrix is orthogonal with determinant 1. Rotations about different axes usually don’t commute, so their order matters.

Aircraft orientation is often given as three angles: yaw \(\psi\) (turning left or right, about the vertical \(z\)-axis), pitch \(\theta\) (nose up or down, about \(y\)) and roll \(\phi\) (tilting the wings, about \(x\)), applied in the aircraft’s own frame, which gives the matrix \(\M{R}_z(\psi)\M{R}_y(\theta)\M{R}_x(\phi)\). 8.7 In practice

  • Aircraft, drones and robots describe which way they are facing by three rotations about axes, applied in a fixed order. The order is part of the definition, and getting it wrong is a classic error.
  • Chemistry and materials science classify crystals and molecules by the rotations and reflections that leave them looking the same.
  • Engineering drawing and CAD use projections onto planes to produce the front, side and top views of a part.
  • Computer vision: a phone that stitches photos into a panorama works out the rotation of the camera between shots as a \(3 \times 3\) orthogonal matrix.

8.8 Going further

  • There is a formula, called Rodrigues’ formula, for the matrix of a rotation by any angle about any axis. Animation and flight software often use quaternions instead, a four-number description of a rotation that blends smoothly between orientations and avoids a problem with describing orientation by three angles, where two of the axes can line up and a direction of movement is lost (Chapter 34).
