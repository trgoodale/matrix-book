13  LU decomposition

Core

NoteIn this chapter you will
  • factorise a square matrix as \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\) by recording the multipliers of Gaussian elimination
  • solve \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) from the factors, by forward and back substitution
  • explain why factorising once saves time when there are many right-hand sides
  • use the factorisation \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\) that software computes, with its row swaps

Builds on Chapter 3, Chapter 11.

Gaussian elimination (Chapter 11) computes a multiplier for every entry it clears, uses it once, and throws it away. This chapter keeps them. Stored in a matrix \(\M{L}\), the multipliers turn elimination into a factorisation \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\): the matrix written as a product of a lower triangular matrix and an upper triangular one. It is the form in which every serious solver actually does elimination, because once \(\M{A}\) has been factorised, each new right-hand side can be solved in a tiny fraction of the time.

13.1 Build intuition

The row reduction stepper is now in LU mode. It carries out ordinary elimination on the right, where \(\M{A}\) gradually turns into \(\M{U}\). Each multiplier it uses is written into \(\M{L}\) on the left, in the position of the entry it cleared.

  • In Needs a swap, a zero pivot forces a row swap. Watch what happens to the multipliers already in \(\M{L}\), and to the third matrix, \(\M{P}\), that appears.

13.2 The ideas

Elimination is a factorisation

Take \[ \M{A} = \mat{2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9}. \] Elimination uses three multipliers: \(R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1\) and \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 4R_1\) clear column 1, then \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 3R_2\) clears column 2, leaving the upper triangular \[ \M{U} = \mat{2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2}. \] Put each multiplier \(m_{ij}\) in row \(i\), column \(j\) of a matrix with 1s on the diagonal and zeros above it: \[ \M{L} = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1}. \] Then \(\M{L}\M{U} = \M{A}\), as you can check by multiplying.

Here is why it works. Elimination finishes each row of \(\M{U}\) by subtracting multiples of the rows of \(\M{U}\) above it. Row 3, for example, became row 3 of \(\M{A}\) minus \(4 \times\) (row 1 of \(\M{U}\)) minus \(3 \times\) (row 2 of \(\M{U}\)). Turned round, \[ \text{row 3 of } \M{A} = 4 \times (\text{row 1 of } \M{U}) + 3 \times (\text{row 2 of } \M{U}) + 1 \times (\text{row 3 of } \M{U}), \] which is row 3 of \(\M{L}\) times \(\M{U}\), by the row view of multiplication (Chapter 3). The same holds for every row. So:

ImportantLU decomposition

If Gaussian elimination on a square matrix \(\M{A}\) needs no row swaps, then \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\), where \(\M{U}\) is the upper triangular result of elimination and \(\M{L}\) is the lower triangular matrix with 1s on its diagonal and the multipliers \(m_{ij}\) below it.

A proof is in Section C.7. If \(\M{A}\) is invertible, these factors are the only ones of this shape.

Solving with the factors

To solve \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\), write it as \(\M{L}(\M{U}\vect{x}) = \vect{b}\) and split it into two triangular systems, with \(\vect{y} = \M{U}\vect{x}\):

  1. solve \(\M{L}\vect{y} = \vect{b}\) by forward substitution, from the top down;
  2. solve \(\M{U}\vect{x} = \vect{y}\) by back substitution, from the bottom up, as in Chapter 11.

Forward substitution is back substitution turned upside down. Since \(\M{L}\) has 1s on its diagonal, the first equation gives \(y_1 = b_1\) directly, and each later one gives \[ y_i = b_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \ell_{ij}\,y_j. \] In fact, \(\vect{y}\) is exactly what the right-hand side would have turned into if it had been carried along during elimination. Forward substitution just replays the stored multipliers on it.

NoteExample 1: two right-hand sides

Use the factors of \(\M{A} = \mat{2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9}\) found above to solve \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\) for \(\vect{b} = (4, 10, 24)\T\) and for \(\vect{b} = (1, 1, 1)\T\).

Solution. For \(\vect{b} = (4, 10, 24)\T\), forward substitution with \(\M{L}\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} y_1 &= 4 \\ y_2 &= 10 - 2(4) = 2 \\ y_3 &= 24 - 4(4) - 3(2) = 2, \end{aligned} \] and back substitution with \(\M{U}\) gives \(2x_3 = 2\), so \(x_3 = 1\); then \(x_2 + x_3 = 2\), so \(x_2 = 1\); then \(2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 4\), so \(x_1 = 1\). So \(\vect{x} = (1, 1, 1)\T\).

For \(\vect{b} = (1, 1, 1)\T\), the factors are reused, and there is no elimination to repeat. Forward substitution gives \(y_1 = 1\), \(y_2 = 1 - 2 = -1\) and \(y_3 = 1 - 4 - 3(-1) = 0\). Back substitution gives \(x_3 = 0\), \(x_2 = -1\) and \(2x_1 - 1 = 1\), so \(x_1 = 1\): \(\vect{x} = (1, -1, 0)\T\). Check: \(\M{A}(1, -1, 0)\T = (2 - 1, 4 - 3, 8 - 7)\T = (1, 1, 1)\T\). ✓

Why factorise? Many right-hand sides

Factorising \(\M{A}\) costs about \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) arithmetic operations, the same as elimination. But the two triangular solves cost only about \(n^2\) each. So once \(\M{A}\) is factorised, solving for a new right-hand side is cheap. For \(n = 1000\), factorising takes about 700 million operations, and each solve afterwards only about 2 million: over 300 times less.

This matters because many problems need the same matrix with many right-hand sides:

  • a structure analysed under many different loads, where the matrix describes the structure and each \(\vect{b}\) is one set of loads;
  • a simulation that moves forward in time, solving a system with the same matrix at every time step;
  • computing \(\M{A}^{-1}\M{B}\) for a matrix \(\M{B}\), one column at a time.

It is also the right way to think about inverses. Instead of computing \(\M{A}^{-1}\) and multiplying, factorise \(\M{A}\) once and solve whenever a product \(\M{A}^{-1}\vect{b}\) is needed (Chapter 7).

Row swaps: \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\)

If elimination meets a zero pivot, it must swap rows, and then \(\M{A}\) itself has no \(\M{L}\M{U}\) factorisation. The fix is to do all the swaps first, in effect. A permutation matrix \(\M{P}\) is the identity matrix with its rows in a different order, and \(\M{P}\M{A}\) is \(\M{A}\) with its rows reordered in the same way. For example, swapping rows 1 and 2 of a \(3 \times 3\) matrix is \[ \M{P} = \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}. \] Every invertible matrix has a factorisation \[ \M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}. \] When a swap happens during elimination, the multipliers already stored in \(\M{L}\) for the two rows swap with them, so that each multiplier stays with its row. The app shows this in Needs a swap.

Software always swaps rows, even when no pivot is zero: at each step it brings up the row with the largest entry in the pivot column. This is called partial pivoting, and it keeps rounding errors under control (Chapter 25). So the factors that software returns are usually not the ones you find by hand, but they are equally valid. To solve \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\), apply the same reordering to \(\vect{b}\): solve \(\M{L}\vect{y} = \M{P}\vect{b}\), then \(\M{U}\vect{x} = \vect{y}\).

NoteExample 2: a factorisation with a row swap

Find \(\M{P}\), \(\M{L}\) and \(\M{U}\) with \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\) for \(\M{A} = \mat{0 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 5}\), and use them to find \(\det\M{A}\).

Solution. The first pivot is 0, so swap rows 1 and 2. Then eliminate: \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 2R_1\) (multiplier 2) and, in column 2, \(R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_2\) (multiplier 1). Row 2 already has a 0 in column 1, so its multiplier is 0. \[ \mat{0 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 5} \xrightarrow{R_1 \,\leftrightarrow\, R_2} \mat{1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 5} \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 - 2R_1} \mat{1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 3} \xrightarrow{R_3 \,\leftarrow\, R_3 - R_2} \mat{1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2}. \] So \[ \M{P} = \mat{0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1}, \] \[ \M{L} = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 1}, \] \[ \M{U} = \mat{1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2}. \] Check row 3 of \(\M{L}\M{U}\): \(2(1, 1, 1) + 1(0, 2, 1) + 1(0, 0, 2) = (2, 4, 5)\), which is row 3 of \(\M{P}\M{A}\). ✓ There was one swap, so \[ \det\M{A} = -(1 \times 2 \times 2) = -4. \]

Determinants for free

Since \(\det\M{L} = 1\) (it is triangular with 1s on the diagonal) and \(\det\M{U}\) is the product of its diagonal entries (Chapter 6), \[ \det\M{A} = \pm\, u_{11}u_{22}\cdots u_{nn}, \] with a minus sign if an odd number of row swaps were made. This is how software computes determinants.

13.3 In code

SciPy, the scientific companion to NumPy, has the factorisation in scipy.linalg. Watch out for one difference of convention: SciPy’s lu returns \(\M{P}\), \(\M{L}\), \(\M{U}\) with \(\M{A} = \M{P}\M{L}\M{U}\), so its \(\M{P}\) is the transpose of the \(\M{P}\) in \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\). MATLAB’s lu follows the convention of this chapter.

import numpy as np

factors = sl.lu_factor(A)          # the expensive step, done once

The saving is real. Here 100 right-hand sides are solved for a \(1000 \times 1000\) matrix, first by solving from scratch each time, then by factorising once:

import time
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, k = 1000, 100
M = rng.standard_normal((n, n))
B = rng.standard_normal((n, k))

t0 = time.perf_counter()
for j in range(k):
    np.linalg.solve(M, B[:, j])
t1 = time.perf_counter()
f = sl.lu_factor(M)
for j in range(k):
    sl.lu_solve(f, B[:, j])
t2 = time.perf_counter()
print(f"solve each time: {t1 - t0:.2f} s,  factorise once: {t2 - t1:.2f} s")

For many right-hand sides, either keep L, U and P and reuse them as above, or use decomposition, which stores the factors and reuses them each time you use backslash:

dA = decomposition(A, 'lu');

13.4 Check your understanding

TipEliminating a \(3 \times 3\) matrix used the multipliers \(m_{21} = 3\), \(m_{31} = -1\) and \(m_{32} = 2\), with no swaps. What is \(\M{L}\)?

\(\M{L} = \mat{1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & 1}\).

TipWhy is forward substitution with \(\M{L}\) so quick?

\(\M{L}\) is lower triangular, so its first equation has one unknown, its second has two, and so on. Each unknown is found directly from the ones before it, with no elimination: about \(n^2\) operations in all.

TipDoes \(\mat{0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0}\) have an LU decomposition?

Not without a row swap: its first pivot is 0. With \(\M{P} = \mat{0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0}\), \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{I} = \M{I}\M{I}\), so \(\M{L} = \M{U} = \M{I}\).

13.5 Key ideas

  • Gaussian elimination factorises \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\): \(\M{U}\) is the echelon form, and \(\M{L}\) is lower triangular, holding the multipliers.
  • To solve \(\M{A}\vect{x} = \vect{b}\), solve \(\M{L}\vect{y} = \vect{b}\) by forward substitution and then \(\M{U}\vect{x} = \vect{y}\) by back substitution.
  • Factorising costs about \(\tfrac{2}{3}n^3\) operations, but each new right-hand side then costs only about \(2n^2\), so factorise once and reuse the factors.
  • Software swaps rows as it goes, and computes \(\M{P}\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\), where \(\M{P}\) records the swaps.
  • The determinant comes for free: the product of the pivots, with a sign for the swaps.

13.7 In practice

  • Structural engineering: a finite element model of a building or bridge leads to one large matrix, fixed by the structure, and one right-hand side for each combination of loads (wind, snow, traffic, people). The matrix is factorised once and every load case is solved from the factors.
  • Circuit simulation and other time-stepping methods: programs that simulate how a circuit or a heat flow changes over time often solve a system with the same matrix at every time step. They factorise it once at the start.
  • Behind the scenes: np.linalg.solve, MATLAB’s backslash and det all compute an LU factorisation with partial pivoting, using the LAPACK library.

13.8 Going further

  • Software stores \(\M{L}\) and \(\M{U}\) together in one array, overwriting \(\M{A}\): the 1s on the diagonal of \(\M{L}\) need no storage. This is what scipy.linalg.lu_factor returns.
  • There are other ways to arrange the same computation. The Doolittle method computes \(\M{L}\) and \(\M{U}\) directly, entry by entry, from \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\); the Crout method puts the 1s on the diagonal of \(\M{U}\) instead.
  • For a symmetric matrix with positive pivots (a positive definite matrix), elimination can be arranged so that \(\M{U} = \M{L}\T\), giving the Cholesky factorisation \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{L}\T\) with half the work and half the storage (Chapter 20).
  • For large sparse matrices, those that are mostly zeros, the factors can fill in with many new non-zeros. Reordering the rows and columns cleverly keeps them sparse (Chapter 26). Why row swaps are needed for accuracy, not just for zero pivots: Chapter 25.
  • Proof that elimination without swaps gives \(\M{A} = \M{L}\M{U}\), and that the factors are unique: Section C.7.
